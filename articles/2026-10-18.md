Some brands do not need an introduction. Horlicks is one of them.

It lives in the kitchen cupboard, usually beside the sugar and slightly behind our good intentions. A tall jar, a stubborn lid, a spoon that went in clean and came out sticky. For a great many Indian households that jar was simply there, like the calendar from the neighbourhood sweet shop. Not every household, of course. Some grew up on Bournvita, some on Boost, some on plain milk and a stern aunt. But the name needs no explaining, and that is a commercial asset most launches would pay handsomely for.

![An older Horlicks jar. The cupboard version of the brand.](images/horlicks-old-jar.jpg)

Which is why the ₹20 milkshake is worth a look. A new brand with a chilled drink must first say who it is. Horlicks only has to say what has changed.

In June 2026, Hindustan Unilever launched Horlicks Milkshake: ready to drink, chilled, in three flavours (Classic Malt, Chocolate, Kesar Badam), priced at ₹20, fortified with 10 nutrients and, by the company's own wording, 40 per cent less total sugars than "a leading similar beverage". The campaign line is "No Guilty, Only Pleasure". The audience, said plainly, is Gen Z.

![Twenty rupees, three flavours. (Ad: HUL)](images/horlicks-milkshake-20-ad.jpg)

The bottle is the trigger. The real subject is what a name built over decades can still do when the product around it changes shape.

## What a name is worth

A legacy gives a brand a few things for free:

A/. Recognition. Nobody has to discover Horlicks.
B/. Habit. Repeated household purchases turn a product into a line on the monthly list.
C/. Inheritance. Adults who met the brand as children may buy it for their own.
D/. Reassurance. A familiar jar feels dependable even when nobody has read the label.

Now notice what is missing from the list: a reason to buy it again. Nostalgia gets a brand noticed. Equity is what remains when the novelty has gone, and it is made of taste, value, availability and whether the product still fits a life that has changed. Memory can take a brand to the shelf once. It cannot take it back a second time.

![Nostalgia, sold to Gen Z. (Campaign: HUL)](images/horlicks-sip-our-way-back.jpg)

There is another wrinkle. In April 2024, the Ministry of Commerce told e-commerce platforms to stop filing drinks like these under "health drinks", since food law does not define the term. HUL renamed its category "functional nutrition drinks". The brand that built its name on health has been asked to stop saying so. Read the new campaign with that in mind: pleasure first, nutrition in the small print. One may call it adaptation. One may also call it a change of subject.

![One health drink, three benefits. The phrase e-commerce was told to retire. (Creative: Horlicks)](images/horlicks-one-health-drink.jpg)

Look at the new carton and the change is already printed on it. It does not call itself a health drink. It calls itself a dairy based drink, a proprietary food, to be taken alongside a regular diet and exercise plan. A hundred and thirty millilitres, and not one claim it cannot defend.

So the question is not whether Horlicks is remembered. It is whether Horlicks is a product, a habit or a familiar promise of nourishment. The milkshake will show which.

## Five jars, five promises

These brands have never competed on the same emotion, which is why they have coexisted for so long.

Horlicks sells the household ritual: classic malt, the family cupboard, the mother's judgement. Its recent moves are all attempts to stretch that ritual into new occasions.

Elsewhere the same name sells a quieter promise. These two posters, which I could not trace to an Indian campaign, treat Horlicks as the end of the day: a mug, a deep breath, the worries put down. The Indian pitch has long leaned the other way, towards growth, strength and the school year.

![Take a moment. A bedtime Horlicks from another market.](images/horlicks-take-a-moment.jpg)

![Goodbye worries, farewell stress. Another market, another promise.](images/horlicks-goodbye-worries.jpg)

Bournvita, in India since 1948, sells taste. Chocolate, a Cadbury halo, a drink the child asks for and the parent pays for. That old negotiation at the counter has done more for the brand than any scientific claim. It has also been the brand under the harshest scrutiny. In 2024 its maker dropped the "health drink" label after a viral video, and in August 2026 the food regulator flagged its growth and height claims, which its maker withdrew from e-commerce.

Boost sells aspiration. The brand launched in 1977, trailed its rivals for years, then in 1985 put Kapil Dev on screen with a line every Indian of a certain age can finish: "Boost is the secret of my energy." Sachin Tendulkar joined in the early 1990s, and research reportedly found children found him too distant, so "my" gradually became "our". Differentiation by ambition rather than ingredient list.

Complan plays in the same sweet and familiar territory as Bournvita, with chocolate and kesar badam on the jar, and its label leads with milk protein, which makes the current protein craze oddly familiar ground. It is the quiet one of the family, now owned by Zydus Wellness.

Milo sells sport and energy. Nestlé launched it in India in August 2019 as a cocoa-malt drink meant to get children playing. Many of us remember free gifts and promotions attached to such drinks. I could not verify them for Milo, so they stay out. A memory is a lead, not a source.

The point is not who wins. It is that each brand owns a different feeling, and the feeling decides what a new product is allowed to be.

## The scoreboard, with footnotes

Now the numbers. A warning first: these are different years, different measures and, in places, different definitions. They are snapshots, not a league table.

A/. Powdered health drinks, share. Industry estimates reported in April 2024 put Horlicks at roughly 44 per cent, Bournvita in double digits, Boost and Complan near 5 per cent each. Horlicks and Boost together, both HUL, come to roughly 49 per cent of the category by the same estimates. Complan's own maker, citing Nielsen, puts it at 4.3 per cent in the year to March 2024, ranked fifth, and at 4 per cent, ranked fourth, in the year to March 2026. The two counts may define the category differently, so the climb should not be over-read.

B/. Horlicks, in rupees. Where a share is not published, turnover helps. Trade reports in April 2024, relaying HUL's investor presentation, said Horlicks had surpassed ₹2,000 crore in turnover. They did not say for what period, and HUL does not publish the figure regularly, so treat it as a marker, not a measurement.

C/. Boost, in rupees. In April 2024, Boost was reported to be on the verge of joining the ₹1,000 crore club. In June 2026, HUL said it had crossed. Small share, serious money, and a movement you can actually trace.

D/. The category itself. In the year to March 2024, the nutrition drink category grew about 6.4 per cent by Nielsen's count, and Complan, its maker says, held its share. In May 2025, HUL's finance chief said average household consumption of these drinks had fallen and that both the category and HUL's products had declined. Then the turn: HUL reports double-digit growth in Lifestyle Nutrition in both the March and June 2026 quarters, and calls the Horlicks relaunch "encouraging".

E/. Bournvita. No brand-level figures are public.
F/. Ready-to-drink protein. No 44 here. One independent audit counted over forty brands, among them Amul, Epigamia and Yoga Bar. Horlicks Protein sits in the mainstream ₹75 to ₹100 band, and HUL has published no figures for it.

So is Horlicks in the game? Yes. It leads the powder category by a distance, it came out of a weak patch with growth, and it is building a second business in bottles. Is it going in the right direction? Probably. But leadership in a category whose household consumption was falling is a lead in a shrinking room, and the new growth is coming from new formats whose numbers nobody outside HUL has seen.

## From the cupboard to the commute

A powder needs a kitchen, a glass, milk and ten spare minutes. A chilled bottle needs a hand.

The potential occasions:

A/. A drink on the commute or between lectures.
B/. A gap between meetings.
C/. A buyer who would never prepare a drink at home.
D/. An impulse purchase at a shop counter or on a quick-commerce app.
E/. An existing adult consumer who wants the familiar name in a portable form.

The strategic shift is quiet but important: from changing the consumer to changing the occasion. Horlicks does not have to convert a stranger. It has to be the name picked at a moment when the shopper would otherwise pick something else.

![The commute as a shelf. (Metro wrap: HUL)](images/horlicks-metro-wrap.jpg)

Also notice that this is not a one-off. It is the third chilled drink from the same house in a handful of months, and each is aimed at a different person.

A/. Boost went ready-to-drink nationally earlier this year, in Classic, Caramel and Badam. Boost is the energy brand, the one with the cricketers, so this is the schoolchild and the sporty teenager in a bottle.
B/. Horlicks Protein arrived in the March quarter, in four flavours: Chocolate, Vanilla, Peanut Butter and a coffee one cheerfully named Proffee. Twenty grams of protein in a 240 ml bottle. This one is for the adult who counts grams.
C/. The ₹20 milkshake is for the person who counts rupees and wants a treat.

Three bottles, three wallets, one parent company. One could call it a ladder, with each rung holding its own buyer. But every ladder has a question. A ₹20 milkshake drinker who graduates to protein is brand growth. A protein buyer who trades down to the ₹20 bottle is not.

And the fridge is crowded. The milkshake's rival is not the jar in the cupboard. It is flavoured milk, cold coffee, a packaged lassi, anything else a thirsty person can grab for the price of a ₹20 note. Does the milkshake create a new occasion, or does it move an old one into a bottle? Powder-at-breakfast and bottle-at-4 pm are different moments, which is encouraging. Encouraging, however, is not evidence.

## Familiarity, convenience, and a small price

The hypothesis is simple:

A/. Familiarity: a name people already trust.
B/. Convenience: no preparation, no spoon.
C/. Accessible trial: ₹20.

Twenty rupees is not a price. It is a permission slip. Chhoto bottle, boro bhorsha, as one might say in Howrah: small bottle, big trust. It lowers the psychological barrier because the name is known, and the financial barrier because the cost is small.

![Small price, old habit: the ₹5 sachet.](images/horlicks-5-rupee-sachet.jpg)

But a permission slip gets you through the door once. Whether affordability holds across different buyers is another matter. And none of this proves the campaign has worked. It proves only that the first sip has been made easy.

## The economics: more demand, or the same demand in a new bottle?

This is where the teardown earns its keep. A rise in milkshake sales is not the same as a rise in demand for Horlicks. Four things could be happening, and they look identical in the first month:

A/. Incremental demand. New buyers try the brand because the format suits them, and existing buyers drink it at moments they previously bought nothing. Brand sales rise and the customer base widens.
B/. Cannibalisation. Powder buyers switch some purchases to the bottle. Milkshake sales look strong, total brand sales barely move.
C/. Trial without retention. Cheap, curious, bought once. Initial sales, no repeat.
D/. Sales without profit. Packaging, chilled distribution, retail margins and promotion eat the additional revenue. Higher turnover, no improvement in profit.

![Classic Malt, on ice. (Photo: HUL)](images/horlicks-classic-malt.jpg)

A word on theory, since this is a marketing blog and not a lecture. When a product's own price changes, you move along its demand curve. When tastes change, the curve shifts. A new format muddies the picture, because it may create a new market, lift the wider brand or simply substitute for an existing product. So one should not casually announce that the milkshake has "shifted demand" for powdered Horlicks. The only meaningful question is whether total demand for the brand has risen once substitution between formats has been netted out.

What does the public record say? The milkshake launched on 2 June, a month before the June quarter ended, and HUL does not publish figures for it. HUL describes "encouraging traction" for Horlicks Superfoods and ready-to-drink in general, which bundles everything together. So anyone who calls the milkshake a triumph or a flop is either HUL's PR team or guessing.

What would settle it:

A/. Incremental sales across the full Horlicks portfolio.
B/. Trial-to-repeat conversion, and how often repeats happen.
C/. Share of buyers who are new to the brand.
D/. Cannibalisation of powdered products.
E/. Distribution reach: is the bottle actually in the fridge?
F/. Contribution margin after variable costs.
G/. Marketing and sampling spend.
H/. Incremental profit, not revenue.

Without these, any claim about success is theatre. Revenue is a mood. Profit is a fact.

## The protein question

Horlicks is also remodelling itself, and protein is where the work shows. The milkshake is aimed at Gen Z, and HUL says so. Protein is framed differently: urban buyers, everyday nutrition, and a campaign idea that an ordinary day is already a workout. HUL has not called it a Gen Z product. But one may fairly guess that many early buyers will be young, the same commuters reaching into the same chiller. Call it the milkshake's older, more serious sibling.

![Twenty grams of protein, plus fibre, in a 240 ml bottle. (HUL)](images/horlicks-protein-fibre.jpg)

HUL describes India's protein market as large but barely tapped: about ₹7,000 crore, with only around 3 per cent of households buying. Its research found four hurdles. Many people do not think they need more, some fear it will not suit them, many link protein with poor taste, and some are unsure whom to trust. The company wants buyers to drink it at least five days a week. That is not a trial. That is a habit, and habit is what this whole piece is about.

Horlicks has talked to adults before. Its Lite range was pitched at "active adults", no added sugar, with "high protein" printed on the box. Protein is a louder second attempt at an older idea.

![Lite Horlicks: nutrition "for active adults", years before protein was a trend. (Pack: Horlicks)](images/horlicks-lite-active-adults.jpg)

Horlicks Protein comes in two forms, and they are not made the same way. The powder, in 36 gram sachets, uses fermented yeast protein. The ready-to-drink bottle, according to the brand's own FAQ, is made with milk protein, casein and whey. So the choice was not a verdict against whey. It was a choice about the powder.

![Zero guilt, no amino spiking. (HUL)](images/horlicks-protein-zero-guilt.jpg)

Notice the vocabulary on the bottle. The milkshake promises "No Guilty, Only Pleasure". The protein shake says "Zero Guilt". Two bottles, one word. HUL's whole chiller is selling absolution, and what needs absolving is sugar. The shake also promises "no amino spiking", a phrase from the supplement industry, where protein counts have long been padded with cheap amino acids. It answers a doubt most shoppers never voiced aloud, which is rather the doubt HUL's own research found: whom to trust.

Why yeast for the powder? HUL's stated reason is lactose. By its own research, more than 60 per cent of Indian adults are lactose intolerant, and about 82 per cent in parts of South India. Those are company figures, not an independent survey, but they explain the design: a protein for the many who find milk difficult. HUL also points to taste, which went through many rounds of formulation, and to the quality and completeness of the protein, which it says carries all nine essential amino acids. The powder adds a prebiotic fibre and a digestive enzyme.

Cost is the second, unstated story. Whey is not cheap, and it is not available to everyone at a friendly price. India imports an estimated 80 to 90 per cent of its supplement-grade whey. Imported concentrate reportedly rose from roughly ₹700 to 800 a kilo in 2024 to about ₹2,300 to 3,000 by mid-2026, and isolate from ₹2,200 to about ₹3,400. A weaker rupee and import duties did the rest. HUL has not said any of this drove its choice. One may only note that the timing was kind, and that a powder which does not depend on imported whey is a powder that can be priced for more people. Which protein is better is a question for nutritionists and the label, not this column. Protein needs differ, and anyone with a kidney condition should ask a doctor.

Then there is the price. The powder works out at about ₹2,500 a kilo, and HUL's own telling is that it could have charged double or more and chose not to. The bottle lists at ₹120 on the brand's own site, and one independent store audit saw it at ₹89. Next to protein shakes, that is the mainstream tier. Next to a ₹20 milkshake, it is a different planet. By my arithmetic from the stated sizes and prices, the 130 ml carton works out to about ₹15 per 100 ml, the 240 ml protein bottle at ₹120 to about ₹50. In that same audit, most buyers set their ceiling below ₹120, so the top rung of the ladder sits at the edge of what people will pay.

Why not put protein on the ₹20 bottle? Because that bottle would then have to answer all four of those doubts in the three seconds before the shopkeeper hands over the change, and because a brand that tries to be indulgent, nutritious and athletic in one bottle will find the label has to choose.

Kindly note: a claim is easier to make on a hoarding than to carry in a bottle.

## The people holding the bottle

Not every buyer is the same buyer.

A/. The existing Horlicks consumer wants something portable and will forgive a lot if it tastes familiar.
B/. The younger buyer cares about taste, price and whether it looks right in a hand. Nostalgia is the weakest card here, since the brand is being sold to people for whom it is not a childhood memory but a name on a shelf.
C/. The parent cares about what the label says and what the child will actually drink.
D/. The shopper deciding between this and flavoured milk is judging value, nothing else.

A caution on nutrition. The ten nutrients and the lower sugar are the company's claims, and "40 per cent less than a leading similar beverage" is a relative statement with an unnamed comparator. Less sugar than something is not the same as little sugar. A familiar brand is not clinical evidence, and a milkshake is not a substitute for a balanced meal or a remedy for anything.

![Less sugar than something. The comparator stays unnamed. (HUL)](images/horlicks-less-sugar-claim.jpg) The honest version of this product is a tasty, convenient drink with some added nutrients. It does not need to be more than that, and it is safer if it never pretends to be.

## What Horlicks must get right

A/. Taste: does it appeal to people who never liked classic malt in the first place?
B/. Price: can ₹20 work as a trial price and as a viable unit of business?
C/. Distribution: is it there, cold and consistent, when the thirst arrives?
D/. Positioning: can it talk about enjoyment without muddling its nutritional role?
E/. Repeat: does the second purchase need no reminder?
F/. Portfolio: can it add consumption without simply moving profitable sales from one format to another?

A good campaign earns awareness and trial. Everything after that belongs to the product and the arithmetic.

## My take

The milkshake is a small innovation that tests a large idea: can emotional equity built over decades let a brand show up at more moments of an ordinary day?

Its advantage is not nostalgia alone. It is the combination of recognition, a place in the shopper's mind, and a format that finally suits modern routines. The protein range adds a second bet, that the same trust can stretch from a treat to a habit.

But the launch should not be judged by social-media buzz, first-month sales or how many people will try something priced at ₹20. The decisive question is whether it produces demand that is incremental, repeatable and profitable across the whole portfolio.

If it does, Horlicks will have done more than add a product. It will have taken an old relationship into a new occasion. If it does not, the milkshake stays a good trial-generation exercise that left the brand's trajectory exactly where it found it.

Either way, the first number worth asking for is not sales. It is the second purchase.

## Closing footnote

A familiar taste. A different occasion.

Perhaps that is the real opportunity. The kitchen cupboard built the habit. Convenience could carry it out of the house.

The ₹20 milkshake is an invitation to revisit an old relationship in a new setting. Whether that turns into repeat purchases and profit remains to be seen.

After all, a legacy can earn a brand its first sip. Only the product can earn the next one.
