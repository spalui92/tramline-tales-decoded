I held a Bible before I had any idea what the Bhagavad Gita was. That is what a missionary school does to a child in Bengal. You learn hymns before mantras and grace before meals.

And yet, when I think, I think in Bengali. When I am angry, hurt or ridiculously happy, the words that arrive first are Bengali words. That is not patriotism. It is plain sequencing. I was reading Bengali at home long before an English textbook landed in my school bag, and the first language you read in becomes the room your mind lives in.

![অ আ ক খ. The room every Bengali mind first moved into.](images/bn-alphabet.jpg)

Psychologists have a neat way of explaining this. Studies on bilingual people show that emotional words land softer in a second language. One well-known 2012 study found that people even make less emotional decisions when they think in a language they learned later. Your first language is where your feelings were first named. Everything after is translation.

Which brings me to a question that has been bothering me for a while. If that is true, what happens to a generation whose first language is quietly becoming its second?

## English won. Let us be fair about why.

Today, English-medium schooling is the default in most middle-class Bengali homes. Bengali, if it shows up at all, is the second language, studied for marks. That choice spreads outward. We read our good books in English, listen to English music, and take our cues from whatever smells faintly of the West. If I am being cheeky, it is colonialism with better Wi-Fi.

But I will not pretend the parents got it wrong. English crosses borders. It opens MNC doors, wins interviews, closes deals and gets a startup pitch heard. For anyone running a business, a good grip on the language is often the difference between being noticed and being nodded at.

Choosing English was a rational decision. The trouble is that a rational decision, made by lakhs of families at once, can still add up to a loss nobody intended.

## What Bengali built

It helps to remember what is at stake. For the better part of two centuries, Bengali was not just a language in Bengal. It was the glue. Literature, cinema, theatre, poetry, music, revolutionary pamphlets and the speeches that moved crowds before and after independence: almost all of it was stitched together in Bengali. We were not merely educated. We knew how to make words do work.

Consider Michael Madhusudan Dutt. He spent his youth wanting to be an English poet, and he was gifted enough to have managed it. It did not take. He returned to Bengali, wrote Meghnad Badh Kavya, and later, while living in France, wrote a sonnet that reads like a confession.

|> "হে বঙ্গ, ভাণ্ডারে তব বিবিধ রতন; তা সবে, (অবোধ আমি!) অবহেলা করি…" O Bengal, your treasury holds many jewels, and I, fool that I was, ignored them all. Michael Madhusudan Dutt, Bangabhasha

That is the point worth holding on to. The best of us were fluent in both languages and still came home.

![Apu ran across the kash fields to see the train. Bibhutibhushan wrote it, Ray filmed it, and the world finally saw Apu.](images/bn-pather-panchali.jpg)

Then there is Tagore, without whom any essay on Bengali would be blasphemy. He travelled the world collecting ideas and built Visva-Bharati on the belief that the world could meet in a single nest. Rooted and global at once: the balance this generation keeps missing. As early as 1892, in his essay Shikshar Herpher, he argued that learning through a borrowed language keeps knowledge from ever truly becoming yours.

He proved it himself, rather painfully. He translated Gitanjali into English, and the Nobel Prize in 1913 went to that version. Bengali readers have long felt the English Gitanjali is a thinner book than the original. If the poet himself could not carry all of his own essence across, what chance does a paperback translation have? Translation is a bridge. It is not the house.

He even seemed to know he was writing for strangers in a distant future.

|> "আজি হতে শতবর্ষ পরে কে তুমি পড়িছ বসি আমার কবিতাখানি কৌতূহলভরে?" A hundred years from today, who are you, sitting and reading my poem out of curiosity? Rabindranath Tagore, Chitra (1896)

More than a century on, the honest answer is: fewer of us than he would have hoped, and many in translation.

![The newspaper that carried Tagore's last poem, dictated on 30 July 1941, days before he died at Jorasanko, the house where he was born.](images/bn-last-poem.jpg)

Music made the same leap. Moheener Ghoraguli, formed in Kolkata in 1975 and named after a line from a Jibanananda Das poem, made rock songs in Bengali when nobody had asked for them. They disbanded in 1981 to little fanfare. Two decades after they began, a comeback at the 1995 Kolkata Book Fair and the compilation Abar Bochhor Kuri Pore set off the whole Bangla band wave of the nineties. They were writing for posterity, and posterity eventually showed up.

Even R.D. Burman, the man who taught Bombay to swing, kept a Bengali test kitchen. He composed Puja songs season after season, and many of those melodies were later served to the whole country as Hindi film songs. "Tere Bina Zindagi Se Koi Shikwa" began life as a Puja song, "Jete Jete Pothe Holo Deri." Bengali was the kitchen. Hindi was the restaurant.

## The quiet slide

Nobody announced the decline. It just crept in.

We buy Feluda in English because the Bengali feels like hard work. Many young Bengalis speak the language fluently but read it haltingly, sounding out the যুক্তাক্ষর like a child in Class 2. Their Bengali texting happens in English script. "Kemon acho" has quietly replaced কেমন আছো.

Uttam Kumar and Suchitra Sen are names, not memories, for most people my age and younger. Unless you are studying film at SRFTI or FTII, there is simply no route by which those films reach you.

![Ritwik Ghatak's 1960 classic lent its name to a 2013 film about Ghatak himself. Our greats now return to us mostly as biopics.](images/bn-meghe-dhaka-tara.jpg)

And yet, here is the strange part. The crowds have not vanished. The 2026 Kolkata Book Fair drew a record 32 lakh visitors, about 15 percent more than the year before. Bengali cinema, after a long slump, has had genuine blockbusters: Bohurupi crossed ₹17.25 crore in 2024, and Dhumketu set the box office alight in 2025.

So the problem is not that we have stopped showing up. It is that we show up for the occasion, not the habit. The Boimela has become an outing, the blockbuster an event. The real question is what we carry home, and in which language.

Puja makes it clearer still. It used to be about the Puja. Now it is a five-day production schedule: the shopping, the vacation, the restaurant bookings, the pandal-hopping route. Everything around the Puja, rarely the Puja itself.

![Mahalaya, 4 am. An entire state once set alarms for a radio broadcast. Many still do. Most now stream it at 9.](images/bn-mahalaya-radio.jpg)

The Puja barshiki, those fat Sharadiya annuals we waited for all year, are still printed, but they feel more like collector's editions than the season's main course. The self-cut Puja albums that every para singer once dreamt of have become YouTube singles that vanish by Lakshmi Puja. This is not a Bengal-only story either. Regional Diwali specials in Marathi and Gujarati are facing the same thinning shelf.

Then there is the slang. The para-rock Bengali that boys at the local club trade over cutting chai has crept into how we speak at home and even into band songs. I wince sometimes. But I should admit the language has survived this before. In 1862, Kaliprasanna Singha wrote Hutom Pyanchar Naksha in raw Kolkata street speech and scandalised respectable society.

![The dhunuchi naach. Smoke, dhak and coconut husk, now performed mostly for the phone camera.](images/bn-dhunuchi.jpg)

Every generation has its own para Bengali, and the language has always absorbed it. Slang is not the disease. Indifference is.

## Bangaliana is more than an alphabet

Bengali was never only a language. It was a whole way of living, what we call Bangaliana. And that is slipping too, one plate and one wardrobe at a time.

Start with the plate. For a Bengali, the simplest blessing was always food. Three centuries ago, Bharatchandra gave a boatwoman a prayer that every Bengali mother still understands.

|> "আমার সন্তান যেন থাকে দুধে-ভাতে…" May my children always have milk and rice. Bharatchandra Ray, Annadamangal (1752)

Dal-bhaat was never boring. It was the base on which a hundred small masterpieces sat: shukto, mochar ghonto, thor-chorchori, kochur shaak, posto in a dozen moods. Many dishes our grandmothers made without a recipe sixty years ago now sound like trivia questions.

![Poush Sankranti. The date palm gives the nolen gur, and the gur gives the pithe its soul.](images/bn-pithe.jpg)

And we had our own momo long before momo arrived. It was called dudh puli: a rice-flour dumpling stuffed with coconut and nolen gur, then simmered in milk. Today we queue for steamed and pan-fried, extra mayo. Our dumpling came in kheer. Theirs come with red chutney and a delivery fee.

Interestingly, YouTube channels are now digging these recipes out of old kitchens, and some young people do try them. But time works like silt in a river. Layer by layer, it buries the familiar until, for the next generation, our own food looks exotic by default.

The wardrobe tells the same story. The tant sari, the dhuti-panjabi and the humble gamchha have become costumes, brought out for Poila Baishakh and Puja photoshoots, then folded away.

![Shubho Noboborsho. The one morning a year we all remember we own a panjabi.](images/bn-noboborsho.jpg)

Look further and the list grows. Chhau from Purulia, Rabindra Nritya, Gaudiya Nritya. The group theatre of Bohurupee and Nandikar, and the jatra companies of Chitpur. The paintings of Abanindranath, Nandalal Bose and Jamini Roy, Ramkinkar Baij's sculptures, Narayan Debnath's Handa Bhonda comics that taught a generation to laugh in Bengali. Salil Chowdhury's arrangements, Gauriprasanna Mazumder's lyrics. Baluchari from Bishnupur, Shantipur tant, kantha stitch, Bankura's terracotta horse, dokra, Pingla's patachitra.

All of it still exists. Very little of it is still celebrated. It has been pushed to the back burner, waiting for a handloom expo or a Republic Day tableau.

|> "পুরানো সেই দিনের কথা ভুলবি কি রে হায়?" Will you forget the stories of those old days? Rabindranath Tagore

![In Santiniketan's Ratanpalli, houses get names rather than numbers. One of them is called, simply, "orange sunlight".](images/bn-nameplates.jpg)

The Baul fakir Lalon asked a question two centuries ago that fits our relationship with all of this rather well.

|> "খাঁচার ভিতর অচিন পাখি কমনে আসে যায়?" How does the unknown bird come and go inside the cage? Lalon Fakir

The bird is still in the cage. We have simply stopped noticing it.

## The ironies

On 3 October 2024, the Union Cabinet declared Bengali a classical language. It is a fine honour. It is also faintly ironic: the language was certified classical at the very moment its own people were buying it in translation.

Across the border, Bangladesh celebrates Bengali with a fervour we rarely muster. It is their national and official language, and their 1952 Language Movement is the reason UNESCO recognised 21 February as International Mother Language Day. Their national anthem, by the way, was written by a man from Kolkata.

|> "আমার সোনার বাংলা, আমি তোমায় ভালোবাসি…" My golden Bengal, I love you. Rabindranath Tagore (1905)

What we forget is that Indian Bengalis also died for this language. On 19 May 1961, eleven people were killed in police firing at Silchar station in Assam's Barak Valley, protesting for Bengali's rightful place there. Bangladesh remembers its language martyrs every February. Most of us have never heard of ours.

The final irony is geographic. The Bengalis who celebrate Bengali most loudly today are often the ones who left: NRIs in New Jersey, IT couples in Bengaluru and Pune. They order mishti online, cook a proper Bengali lunch on weekends because weekdays don't allow it, and treat Durga Puja as the one week a year they get to sit in a room full of people who speak like their mothers.

![When shiuli carpeted the courtyard, we knew Ma was on her way. Now a calendar notification tells us.](images/bn-shiuli.jpg)

Distance makes identity deliberate. At home, we assume it, and what we assume, we neglect. Even UNESCO placed Durga Puja in Kolkata on its intangible heritage list in December 2021, honouring it as public art and spectacle. Which is precisely what it has become.

## When did we stop being angry?

Here is the cynical bit. That mad, almost irrational passion for anything Bengali has cooled.

On 25 March 2004, Tagore's Nobel medal and citation were stolen from the museum at Visva-Bharati in Santiniketan, reportedly while the guards were glued to an India-Pakistan cricket match. The medal was never recovered. Sweden sent replicas. We made a film about it, Nobel Chor, and moved on.

I am not saying this as a pseudo-intellectual. I am saying it because there was a time when a loss like that would have left a permanent mark on the Bengali soul. Bengalis once raved about literature the way other people rave about cricket. We argued over poets at dinner and fought over novelists at the tea stall. A stolen Nobel should have been an open wound. Instead, it became a quiz question.

The Bengali who once roared like this has gone quiet.

|> "বল বীর, বল উন্নত মম শির!" Say, O hero, say: my head is held high! Kazi Nazrul Islam, Bidrohi (1922)

And now there is a new twist. The pen may be mightier than the sword, but what happens when the pen no longer needs a broken heart to move? A well-trained language model can produce a passable Bengali poem in seconds, built from everything Tagore and Nazrul ever wrote. Creativity no longer needs a gut-wrenching moment to be triggered.

That is useful in many ways. It is also exactly why the human version matters more, not less. A machine can rearrange words. It cannot grieve a stolen medal. If we stop feeling the loss, we leave the language to something that never could.

## The mangrove problem

Here is where I think the language story and the Kolkata story are really the same story.

The Sundarbans mangroves survive in salt water because the whole ecosystem holds together: roots, tide, mud and creatures in one tangle. Pull out enough of it and the trees go. A language works the same way. It lives where people can earn in it, gather in it, argue in it and fall in love in it.

|> "বাংলার মাটি, বাংলার জল, বাংলার বায়ু, বাংলার ফল…" The soil of Bengal, the water of Bengal, the air of Bengal, the fruit of Bengal. Rabindranath Tagore (1905)

That ecosystem in Kolkata has been eroding for decades. Not because of one party or one government, but because problems that stack up slowly eventually stop looking like problems and start looking like weather. Industry thinned, opportunities thinned, and a city that once led stopped keeping pace with its peers. Ask anyone with three or four years of experience and they will tell you Kolkata underfeeds the ambitious. So they leave for Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune.

It is tempting to say other states do better because their people are more talented. I don't buy that. They built ecosystems that let people stay, and rootedness followed.

Here is the cost we rarely count. Brain drain does not just export workers. It exports the next generation of Bengali readers. The grandparents who told the Thakurmar Jhuli stories stay behind in Kolkata. The grandchildren grow up elsewhere, with English at school, another language on the street, and Bengali only on weekend video calls.

And the pipeline breaks in childhood. Nobody picks up Sunil Gangopadhyay at twenty-five if they never giggled at Sukumar Ray at eight.

|> "আয়রে ভোলা খেয়াল-খোলা, স্বপনদোলা নাচিয়ে আয়…" Come, you carefree dreamer, come swinging on your dreams. Sukumar Ray, Abol Tabol (1923)

![Sonar Kella. For many of us, the first Feluda, read in Bengali, under the covers, long past bedtime.](images/bn-sonar-kella.jpg)

Bengali readers were always built early: Thakurmar Jhuli in 1907, and Sandesh, the children's magazine Upendrakishore Ray Chowdhury started in 1913 and Satyajit Ray revived in 1961. Feluda himself was born to give children something to read in Bengali. Skip that stage, and the adult reader never turns up.

Even the adda moved house. College Street's Coffee House was Bengali's living room, where ideas were argued into shape out loud. Today the adda lives in family WhatsApp groups, mostly as forwards, often in Banglish. The conversation survived. The language thinned.

![College Street. A bookshop open since 1886, and a Coffee House upstairs where Bengal once argued itself into shape.](images/bn-college-street.jpg)

## Bengal is more than Darjeeling and Digha

If Bangaliana needs reviving, tourism might be the most practical place to start, because nothing makes people value something faster than strangers paying to see it.

Ask most people to plan a trip within West Bengal and you will hear two names: Darjeeling and Digha. Perhaps the Sundarbans if they are feeling adventurous. But the state is full of places that have never had their moment.

There are the red laterite hills of Purulia's Ajodhya, where palash sets the slopes on fire every spring. There are Bishnupur's terracotta temples, carved by the Malla kings, and Gangani in Paschim Medinipur, a gorge that locals proudly call Bengal's Grand Canyon. Add the Lepcha villages of Kalimpong, the forests of the Dooars, the old nawabi grandeur of Murshidabad, and Taki on the banks of the Ichamati.

|> "ধনধান্য পুষ্পভরা আমাদের এই বসুন্ধরা, তাহার মাঝে আছে দেশ এক সকল দেশের সেরা…" Our earth, full of grain, wealth and flowers, and within it a land, the finest of all. Dwijendralal Ray

Not every state needs a Goa. But every state has its own untapped Goa, waiting for someone to notice it. I would love to see the day when tourism here is commercialised just enough that an offbeat village nobody has heard of finally gets the attention it deserves.

![Green louvres, stained glass and a chequered floor. Heritage stays may be Bengal tourism's easiest win, and they come with free nostalgia.](images/bn-heritage-room.jpg)

## The ones who didn't get the memo

Before this turns into a eulogy, a word for the people quietly refusing to let it become one.

Sunday Suspense has done for Bengali literature what classrooms could not. Young people who would never pick up a Byomkesh in print will happily listen to one for two hours on a Sunday afternoon. Literature has moved from the page to the ear, and it is still literature.

Streaming platforms like Hoichoi have brought Feluda and Byomkesh back to the screen, and some viewers wander back to the books afterwards. Coke Studio Bangla has made folk songs cool again, though, true to form, that reinvention is coming from across the border.

Rabindra Sangeet on a guitar used to earn raised eyebrows. Today, new renditions are how many young people first meet Tagore. Purists may wince, but as long as the spirit is honest, it keeps the man alive in our conversations, and that is what matters. The little magazines still hold their corner at the Boimela, which is where many young Bengali writers take their first steps.

![A wall asks where Jibanananda would return if the river Dhansiri doesn't survive. Somebody still writes poetry on walls. That counts.](images/bn-jibanananda-wall.jpg)

None of this is a golden era. But it is proof the language still has a pulse. And if the rest of us are slow to join, the young ones have Tagore's permission to keep walking anyway.

|> "যদি তোর ডাক শুনে কেউ না আসে তবে একলা চলো রে…" If no one answers your call, walk on alone. Rabindranath Tagore (1905)

## A small ask

I am not asking anyone to give up English. I would be a hypocrite: I am writing this piece in English, and that is precisely the point. English is the language of our work. Bengali is the language of our homes, our tempers and our lullabies. We need both, the way Madhusudan, Tagore and Ray had both.

So, a small ask, nothing heroic.

A/. One Bengali book a quarter. Start with Feluda if you must. Read it in Bengali, slowly.

B/. One old Uttam-Suchitra film on a Sunday afternoon, with family if possible.

C/. One Sunday Suspense episode on your next long drive.

D/. One forgotten dish from your grandmother's kitchen, cooked badly the first time, better the second.

E/. One weekend somewhere in Bengal that isn't Darjeeling or Digha.

F/. And speak Bengali to the youngest child in your family. Not because it will get them a job, but because it will tell them where they come from.

![Goopy and Bagha got three boons from the king of ghosts. We need just one: the habit.](images/bn-bhooter-raja.jpg)

Celebrating your hometown while shying away from its language cannot both stay true for long. Kolkata is not just a pin code. It is a way of saying things.

॥ "সুন্দরবনের ম্যানগ্রোভ নোনা জলেও শিকড় ছাড়ে না। আমরা নিজের মাটিতে দাঁড়িয়েও পারব না?" The Sundarbans mangroves don't let go of their roots even in salt water. Can't we manage it, standing on our own soil?
