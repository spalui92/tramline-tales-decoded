Zomato shows up in almost every marketing case study, and the reason is simple. It treated its voice as a product surface long before most brands did.

I want to start there, because the AI assistant I tested this week only makes sense once you see what it stands on.

॥ A notification is a feature with a copy deck.

## Brand as a product surface

Early food apps used notifications like billing slips. "Independence Day, 15% off at selected restaurants."

Zomato used the same slot to earn attention. Over time, people opened its messages instead of swiping them away.

![One craving, one price, one line. (Poster as shared online)](images/zomato-crave-it-grab-it.jpg)

Look at that poster again. The restraint is the craft.

Then the voice loosened into Hinglish. "Aaj cooking bandh, Zomato shuru" says what the app does in five words.

![The product promise, in a friend's voice. (Poster as shared online)](images/zomato-cravings-ignore-mat-karo.jpg)

Formats change and the warmth stays. Here a phone becomes the plate.

The red never moves, which is the point. A brand that looks the same everywhere can afford to say different things.

![Hand-lettered, app-store ready. (Poster as shared online)](images/zomato-every-meal-matters.jpg)

Then came the lines that made people stop scrolling. "Doodh mangoge, doodh denge. Kheer mangoge, kheer denge."

It sold the idea of avoiding misheard orders without a hint of pitch.

![A pun that sells a practical benefit.](images/zomato-doodh-kheer.jpg)

On Women's Day, the brand wrote a line that invited a wince and then flipped it with a belan. It made people smile first and think second.

That is how good copy tends to work. The setup does the lifting and the punchline lands it.

![The setup invites a wince, the punchline lands it.](images/zomato-womens-day.jpg)

The collaborations were just as sharp. A Zomato billboard asked whether it should send over some gajar ka halwa, and a Tinder billboard answered from across the road.

It only works when you see both.

![Two brands, two billboards, one joke. (Billboards as shared online)](images/zomato-tinder-gajar-halwa.jpg)

If you work in copywriting, advertising or social media, this is a free education. Half of creative work is inspiration and half is hours put in, and Zomato's output feeds the first half generously.

For a product team, the quiet advantage is bigger. Recall lowers acquisition cost, so you launch into an audience that already likes you.

## The story that funded the long road

For years Zomato had reach and reputation without steady profit. Food delivery front-loads its costs, and the returns arrive slowly.

Brand heat kept the company fundable through that stretch. A product everyone talks about is easier to back than one nobody notices.

![The early pitch: discover more places to eat around you. (Pankaj Chaddah and Deepinder Goyal, early campaign image)](images/zomato-founders-early.jpg)

The numbers show how bumpy it stayed. Eternal, the parent of Zomato and Blinkit, posted a profit of ₹25 crore in the first quarter of FY26, a sharp fall from the year before.

By the fourth quarter it reported ₹174 crore, with Blinkit turning adjusted EBITDA positive. Brand first, margins later, and even now the margins keep moving.

![Every clever line ends with a person carrying a red bag to a door. (Photo: Design Forever)](images/zomato-rider.jpg)

## The problem is latency, not appetite

Picture it. It is 6 pm, dinner has to be on the table by 7:30, and the only constraint you have is a budget.

Thousands of restaurants are a gift until they become the problem. The decision takes longer than the delivery.

In computing terms, ordering has a latency problem, and it lives in the user's head. A product team would find the same pattern in surveys and session recordings: long scrolling, repeated filtering and second guessing.

## The beta, and what I tested

Zomato has experimented with AI before. A chatbot for Gold members arrived in 2023, and a voice ordering pilot has been spotted in pockets of Bengaluru.

What I tested is a newer assistant, still in beta, with its own card at the top of the feed and a sparkle button in the bottom navigation. I cannot tell from outside how it relates to the earlier chatbot, so I will describe only what I saw.

![The assistant gets the top slot in "Recommended for you". (Screenshot: the author, 22 Oct 2026)](images/zomato-assistant-home-screen.jpg)

Read as a system, it did four things.

A/. Intent parsing. I asked for two nearby South Indian places, and it resolved location, cuisine and result count from one sentence.

B/. Constraint filtering. I added a 4+ rating from at least 500 reviewers. It applied both as hard filters and returned two restaurants, one budget friendly and one established chain, neither feeling like a paid push.

C/. Personalisation. On opening a restaurant, it told me this was a place I visit often and listed what I always order. That is retrieval over order history, surfaced at the right moment.

D/. Action inside the flow. It opened the restaurant, locked my menu choice and listed every flat offer with the terms stated correctly. "Flat ₹100 off above ₹399" meant exactly that, and "20% up to ₹75" was explained as a percentage with a cap.

It stopped before the offer tap and the payment. That is a sound product decision, since a human should own the irreversible step.

By my own rough estimate, the session took half the browsing time or less. That is an impression, not a measurement, and it is exactly what a proper test should replace.

![Voice is where this is heading. (Post: The Brihaspati Infotech, on the Bengaluru voice pilot)](images/zomato-voice-bot-pilot.jpg)

## Where it stops today

It is reactive. It executes a clear brief and volunteers nothing.

A/. No cart nudges, such as "add ₹60 to unlock the flat offer".

B/. No scheduling from a sentence.

C/. No order notes to the restaurant, such as "less spicy, no cutlery".

D/. No calorie filtering unless I switch on health mode myself.

E/. Order issues go to Help and a human agent.

For a beta, that is defensible. A narrow, reliable assistant earns trust faster than a broad, flaky one.

## The race it is running in

Zomato is not alone here. Swiggy now lets users order food, groceries and dining through assistants like ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, built on the open Model Context Protocol.

It has also announced voice ordering with Sarvam AI across 11 Indian languages, and launched a health-focused section called EatRight.

So the question is not whether conversational ordering arrives. It is whether Zomato's version, living inside its own app and its own data, is stickier than ordering from someone else's assistant.

## How the assistant moves revenue

Food delivery revenue is orders, multiplied by average order value, multiplied by what the platform earns per order. The assistant can touch each part.

A/. Orders. Fewer abandoned searches means higher session-to-order conversion. My hypothesis is a relative lift of 5 to 10 percent.

B/. Basket size. Cart nudges and offer ranking push people past thresholds. My hypothesis is 3 to 5 percent, and only once those features ship.

C/. Frequency. An easy first order makes the second one likelier. My hypothesis is 3 to 6 percent more monthly orders from repeat users.

D/. Cost to serve. Clear offer terms, order notes and fewer wrong items should reduce support contacts and wasted discount spend.

Here is an illustrative case. Take 100 users ordering four times a month at ₹400, with the platform earning about 20 percent of order value.

Baseline order value is ₹1,60,000, or roughly ₹32,000 in platform revenue. With a 5 percent frequency lift and a 4 percent basket lift, it becomes about ₹1,74,720, and revenue rises by roughly ₹2,900 per 100 users per month.

If inference costs ₹3 to ₹5 per user per month, that is ₹300 to ₹500 per 100 users. The gain survives, but the margin for error is thinner than it looks.

## One scorecard, three layers

Metrics scattered across a dashboard tell no story. I would read them in three layers, each answering a different question.

A/. Revenue: did money move? Session-to-order conversion, average order value, orders per user per month, and incremental revenue per user net of inference cost.

B/. Experience: why did it move? Decision latency (app open to first add-to-cart), query success rate, reformulation rate and repeat assistant use.

C/. Marketing: is it feeding the brand? Personalised notification click-through, reactivation of lapsed users, CSAT or NPS on assistant orders, and organic and brand search lift.

The causal chain is one line. Lower decision latency improves the experience, the experience brings repeat use and word of mouth, and those lead to revenue.

A note on engagement. For a utility, more messages per session can mean the assistant is confused, not loved. Shorter sessions with higher conversion are the good outcome.

Calorie data will appeal to Gen Z and millennial users, for whom food is part of a lifestyle and no longer a pure indulgence. I would track them as their own cohort.

They may order more often but skip desserts and sides, so basket size could dip. If frequency and retention rise while baskets shrink, the feature is a loyalty tool, not a basket builder, and it should be judged that way.

## If the numbers disagree

A launch should come with a diagnosis plan, not only a victory lap.

A/. High trial, low repeat: the first answer is not earning trust. Improve accuracy and show sample prompts.

B/. High reformulation: intent parsing is failing. Review failed queries by cuisine, budget and diet.

C/. Good conversion, flat baskets: ship offer ranking and cart nudges next.

D/. Strong lift, weak unit economics: route simple queries to a smaller model, cache common filters and cap long sessions.

E/. Rising complaints: tighten dietary handling and add confirmation before anything is locked.

## How I would roll it out

Start with a staged release by city, with a holdout group that never sees the assistant. Agree the success rules before launch, so nobody moves the goalposts afterwards.

Judge the launch on incremental revenue per user, net of inference cost. If conversion rises but incremental revenue does not, the assistant is shifting orders around and not adding any.

Beta is the right place for this. It is cheap to learn now and expensive to learn at full scale.

## What could break

A/. Accuracy. One wrong veg or non-veg suggestion damages trust more than ten good answers build it. Dietary constraints need strict handling and honest "I'm not sure" responses.

B/. Language. India orders in Hinglish and regional languages, often mixed in one sentence. Voice that stumbles on accents or code-switching will stay a metro feature.

C/. Partner data. Menus, calorie counts and order notes all depend on restaurants. The assistant is only as reliable as the data feeding it.

D/. Cost. Every session has an inference cost in a business with thin margins. The assistant has to earn that back.

E/. Privacy and consent. "You always order this" delights when it feels like service and creeps when it feels like surveillance. Clear consent and user control matter, especially under India's data protection law.

F/. Ranking neutrality. My two results did not feel like paid placements. If sponsored listings arrive, they need clear labels.

## The people a PM has to rally

None of this ships from one desk. Engineering and ML own accuracy and latency, design owns the conversation flow, and restaurant operations own data quality.

Support owns the handoff to Help, legal owns consent, and marketing owns the story. The product manager's real job is to keep all of them aimed at one metric.

## The roadmap, tied to the numbers

A/. Phase 1, offer ranking and cart nudges. Moves average order value. The assistant already reads the offers, so saying which one wins for your cart is a small step.

B/. Phase 2, saved preferences and order notes. Moves repeat rate and complaint rate. Customers placing ₹300 to ₹500 orders care about "less spicy", and a restaurant that reads the note earns loyalty.

C/. Phase 3, scheduling and group ordering. Moves frequency. "Four people, one vegetarian, one gluten-free, under ₹1,200" is where decision fatigue peaks.

D/. Phase 4, one assistant across Zomato, Blinkit and dining. Moves cross-product usage. The hampers banner on the home screen already hints at gifting.

Each phase adds agency, so each needs stronger guardrails. Confirmations and easy undo matter more as the assistant does more on its own.

Blinkit already knows how to be witty in public. A shared assistant would let that voice follow the customer from dinner to the late-night matchbox.

## The supporting cast

The assistant is the lead, but it stands on features that were already there.

A/. Kilocalorie display and health mode. Per-dish calorie data gives the assistant structured fields to filter on. It also fits how people eat now, since we eat to live and not live to eat.

B/. Highly reordered tag. Social proof that doubles as a ranking signal. It tells a first-timer what regulars trust.

C/. Deep filters. A good assistant is mostly a good filter layer with language on top.

D/. Limited-time flat discount. A small extra discount that lasts a couple of minutes creates urgency without a heavy markdown.

E/. Cancelled order deals. Food from a cancelled order resells at a sharp price, which recovers waste and rewards the customer.

F/. Extra charge on cash on delivery. Cash means handling and reconciliation, so a small fee quietly steers people to UPI or cards.

## The takeaway

Zomato spent years learning to talk like a person, and that goodwill gave it room to ship something ambitious. Now the app is learning to listen.

It is still a beta, so the honest verdict is a hypothesis. The assistant lasts if it stays accurate, pays for its own compute and keeps the user's trust.

Watch decision latency, repeat use and incremental revenue per user. If those three move together, this becomes the default way to order.
