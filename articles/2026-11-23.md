॥ "Kaun kehta hai aadmi apni kismat khud likhta hai?" Who says a man writes his own destiny? The film's tagline, and its question to us.

Most of us have met a tiny, harmless version of this feeling. You call a helpline and a recorded voice offers five options, none of which is yours. You press the nearest one, it leads to another menu, and somewhere around the third menu you realise that the questions were never questions. They were instructions with a polite face.

Now take away the harmless part. Put a real voice at the other end, give it a threat, and give it your family.

That is Aamir (2008), and I think it is a film about trap, terror and trepidation, arriving back to back and never giving him a breath in between. Aamir Ali, played by Rajeev Khandelwal, is the person we watch. The real protagonist, the one with all the agency, is the structure closing around him.

![The question, printed right on the poster, over a very crowded street. (Poster: Aamir, 2008)](images/aamir-poster.jpg)

## A Name Before a Man

Dr. Aamir Ali lands in Mumbai from London, a doctor coming home. He expects a homecoming. What he gets first is an officer who goes through his bags once, then again, and finds nothing.

It is a short scene and it carries the film's politics. Aamir asks whether his bags would have been checked this carefully if his name were Amar.

|> "Would you have treated me thus, had my name been Amar?" Aamir to the officer, as quoted in Outlook India's review

The film was made in the long shadow of 9/11, and I would not read that scrutiny as a verdict on his faith alone. It was a reflex the world had just learnt, from a morning it had just lived through. But a reflex has to land somewhere, and this one landed on a name.

A year later, New York (2009) would look at the same wound from the American side. Aamir looks at it from the way home: a man who left, built a life elsewhere, and is stopped at the one door he expected to be open.

What I admire is that the film keeps its pressures apart. The officer is the state's suspicion. The suspicion we carry in our own heads, the one that fills in the rest of the story the moment it hears a name, is another thing. And the voice on the phone, who speaks the language of faith and duty and tells him to serve Islam instead of living abroad, is something else again: indoctrination, a man in the dark trying to press his own verdict on a man in the light.

Critics at the time objected that the film sets one blameless Muslim against a crowd of menacing ones, and that deserves to be said out loud. My own reading is narrower. Aamir is never made to stand for anyone. He is a doctor, a son, a man with a ticket home, and he carries nobody's community on his back, however hard the day tries to hand him that burden.

## Instructions, Not Questions

Now the trap itself. A phone is pushed into his hand at the airport, a video shows his family held captive, and from there the film turns into a list of orders. Go here. Call this number. Wait. Do not ask why.

![One phone, one voice, and a collar that has already started to give up. (Still: Aamir, 2008)](images/aamir-phone-call.jpg)

He cannot check the video. He cannot trust the people he meets. He cannot say no. Every question the day puts to him has exactly one permitted answer, and that, I would say, is the moment a question stops being a question and becomes a summons.

The screenplay's cleverness is that every attempt to escape tightens the knot. He hides, and hiding puts him in a lodge, and the lodge leads him to a stranger's house and a red briefcase. He fears, quite reasonably, that it holds a bomb. It holds money, which is almost worse, because now he is a courier.

It gets stolen, and he fights to get it back, because as far as he knows, losing it means losing them. The red briefcase is the film's best picture of responsibility without agency. He carries somebody else's purpose, and its contents change without his knowledge.

![Held close, like something that might go off or might be taken. (Still: Aamir, 2008)](images/aamir-red-briefcase.jpg)

The man behind it all stays almost entirely in the dark, and I mean that nearly literally.

|> "Shot almost completely in the dark." Rediff's 2008 review, on the caller

Gajraj Rao gives him a voice that never needs to shout. It is the calm of someone with all the time in the world, and that is the horror of it: he is patient while Aamir runs. There is no cartoon villain here, only a man who sounds certain that he is right.

## The Table in the Dingy Room

Aamir breaks down more than once in this film, but there is one scene I cannot shake.

Somewhere along the route he ends up in a dingy hotel where the light barely finds its way in. A meal is served, and he sits down in front of it. I will remember the sitting more than the sound. The shoulders have given up before the tears arrive, and then he breaks, loudly, for the first time.

Until that moment he has been looking for a way out. He has been running, calling, bargaining and hiding, because a person who still believes in an exit behaves like one. At the table, he and we understand together that there is no emergency exit and no damage control left in his hands.

|> "The helplessness of the protagonist has been captured remarkably on celluloid." Filmibeat's 2008 review

What he wants, I think, is not even the hometown or the family in the strict sense. It is something familiar and comforting, a place where nobody is giving him orders. A plain meal on a table is the nearest thing the day offers, and it only shows him how far away the rest is. It is a dreadful dream coming true in the most ordinary of settings.

## Daylight, Small Money

Here is what I find most unsettling about how the film looks. It is bright.

Grittier thrillers go looking for the dark, and the good ones know why. Think of The Killing, where the rain never stops and the sky never lifts. That overcast sky is not a backdrop but a verdict. Every grey hour tells you how tense the town is and how gruesome the case at hand has been, and the downpour lets each scene land harder than any dialogue could.

Think of Daredevil, which spends most of its life after sundown. Of course it does. It is Hell's Kitchen, and violence reads differently at night. Put that brutality in the dark and the atrocity finally gets its proper stage.

This is what weather and the hour do for a story. If you let a scene be a character, it is not only the actor and the location. It also has a sky and a time of day, and the best directors cast those as carefully as they cast a face.

Aamir casts neither rain nor night. It casts the sun. Twenty-two days, seven in the morning to five in the evening, a purse of two crore, and daylight as the only light on the payroll, working for free.

It is a cruel choice, and I mean that as praise. A trap in the dark can be forgiven for not being seen. A trap in daylight is witnessed by everyone, and a man is slowly cooked in front of one and all while the sun carries on as if nothing is wrong. He cannot say where it hurts, and nothing around him is kind enough to help him fight it. The brightness comforts nobody.

Alphonse Roy's lens is the quiet accomplice here. It stays close enough to catch every flinch and wide enough to show how many people are looking away, and it never blinks at noon. In this film the camera does a lot of the talking, just as the scenes do, and to my eye it earns every full mark a critic gave it.

Small money was part of the bargain. Dark films about terror were made small in those days, and I would not call that a choice, only the habit of the time. Habits push directors toward real streets, small crews and stories that look like the morning papers.

Satya and Black Friday walked that same road, where nobody tries to make the story larger than life. Aamir belongs to it too, and I am less interested in its lenses than in what its limits did. When you cannot build a set, you borrow the day itself, and a borrowed day has no interest in your hero's feelings.

## The Mumbai We Do Not Frame

This is not the Mumbai of Wake Up Sid, which waits for the monsoon because that is when Marine Drive looks its prettiest. This is the Mumbai we least glamorise on screen, mostly because it does not suit everyone's appetite.

It is the underbelly of the city, the part we prefer not to discuss until the day it pokes the commoner in the ribs. A local train stops for the wrong reason, a lane floods, a crowd turns into a crush, and for a moment everyone is reminded of what the postcards leave out.

That too is a natural part of a city, and yet watching it on celluloid makes us lose the plot. It stretches our bandwidth. It is not the Mumbai we are used to, or exposed to, and perhaps not the one we want to get accustomed to either.

|> "A whiff of Mumbai, minus the cliches." Rediff's 2008 review

![Shutters, a stray dog and a crowd at noon. Nobody in this frame is hiding. (Still: Aamir, 2008)](images/aamir-lane.jpg)

Lanes, lodges, markets, crowded transport. Rajeev Khandelwal has spoken about shooting at actual locations, and you can feel it: nobody in those crowds is waiting for a cue.

Popular imagination has long parked the city's shadow economy in its dense, crowded quarters, and Dharavi has become shorthand for all of it. Mainstream cinema has leaned on that shorthand for decades, sometimes placing crime, and its darker cousin terror, there by reflex. I would handle that shorthand with care.

What Aamir does is quieter. It does not tell us where evil lives. It shows us the ordinary lanes where a stranger can be handed a briefcase and nobody blinks.

A crowd is supposed to be company. Here he is surrounded by thousands and entirely alone.

## A Body That Gives Way

Rajeev Khandelwal, in his first film, begins as a man with ordinary hopes, still in suit and tie in the Mumbai heat. By the end he is dishevelled, hollowed out and cornered.

![A man leaning on a shuttered door, looking at nothing. The body keeping count. (Still: Aamir, 2008)](images/aamir-shutter.jpg)

But the film does not hang on him alone. I will admit I could not name most of the supporting cast from memory, and I suspect that is by design. They work like a chain of doors, each one opening onto the next.

There is the whispering man at the airport and the customs officers who go through his bags. There is the cop who chases him after the phone-booth call, and the lame man at the lodge who leads him to the house with the red briefcase. There is the woman at the lodge who helps him when he is beaten, robbed and fighting to get that briefcase back. And hanging over all of them is Gajraj Rao, who is only a voice for most of the film.

None of them is a villain or a friend for long. Each does exactly enough to keep Aamir moving and never enough to free him, and that is how the screenplay passes him from scene to scene. The trap is made of people as much as of plot.

|> "Watch him emote with his eyes in the latter reels mainly." Filmibeat's 2008 review

I agree with that, and I would go a step further. More than the dialogue, it is his expressions and the way each situation squeezes a different shape of helplessness out of him that build the film brick by brick. Fear, pleading, anger, numbness, and then a tiredness so deep it looks like calm.

Watch how he watches, too. He is a doctor, educated abroad, and I suspect he comes from a comfortable and privileged world. Thrown without warning into a city and a situation that have nothing to do with his past or his present, he is a square peg in a round hole. So he does what a doctor does. He observes the faces, the situations and the hours passing, and he compares them the way you would compare symptoms on a chart.

Then there is the scene where he throws up. It was shot in a real location, like everything else in the film, and by the accounts that came out of the shoot, the retching was real.

I think about why a body does that. We throw up when we cannot take in any more, and we throw up to get the toxin out. Like the white cells in our blood, our system fights for us until the point where it cannot. Once it starts to wither, it retaliates, and that retaliation looks like this.

Metaphorically, when people face oppression, they retaliate through demonstration. A man who has been cornered like this cannot take that route. His protest is the only one left to him, and it simmers in his stomach. It is another shape of helplessness captured on screen, and there is a fine line here that deserves underlining: it is not weakness, it is a body regurgitating the truth and scarring its own immunity in the process.

## One Choice, and a Wrong Headline

Then the bus. He is told to leave the briefcase under his seat, and he understands what it now holds. He gets off. And then a young passenger waves at him from the window.

![The bus, and a face that has run out of questions. (Still: Aamir, 2008)](images/aamir-bus.jpg)

It is a small, ordinary wave, and it changes the film. He goes back, pushes through the packed bus, grabs the briefcase and runs, shouting at construction workers to clear out. There is no safe place to put it down, so he holds on to it. The phone rings. For the first time all day, he does not answer, and he smiles.

This is not a triumph, and I would not call it redemption. It is the first choice of the day that is truly his, and he spends all of it on strangers.

Then the film does its cruellest thing. The news calls him a suicide bomber, the very label the day had been pinning on him since the airport. The name that was suspected at the gate is convicted on television.

This scene asks more questions than it answers. A man from the good side of society is pushed to the wrong side, and then tagged as exactly what he never was. Who gets to write the last line about a man once he can no longer speak?

When we give a film two or two and a half hours, we usually want to be sent home warm. This one does not do that, and I would not call it sad. It is harrowing, and I think that was the director's intention: to call a spade a spade, and for once not sugarcoat anything.

Aspirational films ask us to read between the lines. This one gives us the lines and nothing else. It plays almost like a film based on a true event, trying to stay as honest to its subject as it can, and that is why the climax is perhaps the most emotionally harrowing scene in the whole film.

A line from King Lear says what I cannot say as well.

|> "As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods; they kill us for their sport." William Shakespeare, King Lear

## A Film I Remember More Than I Revisit

Some tragic films make good company on a second night. Aamir is not one of them. It begins uneasy, stays tense and ends in heartbreak, with nowhere to rest in between.

I do not think that is a flaw. Some films leave you a story you want to keep. Others leave you an experience you would rather not repeat.

If you liked Satya or Black Friday, this belongs on the same shelf. They are not the same film in size or influence, but they share an appetite for lived-in streets and moral discomfort, and a refusal to make a city or its violence look glamorous.

In The Dark Knight, Alfred says of the Joker that some people want nothing from the world except to watch it burn. Some plans are like that too. They do not need you to win or even to understand, they only need to watch an ordinary person crash and burn.

And yet, for the first time in that whole day, Aamir refuses to play to the gallery. He will not perform the part that was written for him. In the eyes of the people who built the trap, that makes him a traitor and not a hero.

The plan, seen through their lens, derailed. His intentions did not.
