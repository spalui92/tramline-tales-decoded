Before there was Fevicol, there was saresh. Ask any old carpenter. It was a glue made from animal fat and collagen, and it came with a ritual: light a fire, heat a pot, wait for it to melt, and hold your nose. The smell clung to the workshop, the clothes and the evening. It did the job, eventually.

In 1959, Balvant Parekh started Pidilite with an idea so simple it sounds like a joke: glue should come out of a container ready to use. No fire, no pot, no stink. Sixty-seven years later, walk into any hardware shop in Burrabazar and ask for "white glue". The shopkeeper will look at you as if you'd asked for "brown fizzy drink". You want Fevicol, madam. Say so.

![Jodega toote rishtey. (Poster: © Sethi)](images/fevicol-toote-rishtey.jpg)

## The smell was the market

Every great product begins with a pain people have stopped noticing. Saresh was messy, slow, inconsistent and smelly. Carpenters put up with it the way Kolkata puts up with waterlogging in July: grumbling, but resigned. Fevicol was white, clean and ready the moment the lid came off. Nobody needed persuading that this was progress. They only needed one try.

India has seen this film before. Dalda's vanaspati was once sold as the modern answer to ghee. Then refined oils turned up and gave the kitchen a reason to switch again. A substitute wins when the old way hurts, and it keeps winning only until something hurts less. Hold that thought: it comes back later.

Pidilite also worked out that a glue for professionals could become a glue for every home. From 1970 it sold smaller packs, so the adhesive that held a wardrobe together could also rescue a child's school project at eleven at night. One product, two customers, and the second one never asks the price.

## Sell to the hand that holds the chisel

This is the part nobody puts on a hoarding, and it is the real genius.

In India, most furniture isn't bought off a showroom floor. A carpenter builds it, and the carpenter decides what goes into it. The homeowner pays for the glue, never sees it and has never once asked about it. So Pidilite went to the carpenter. Kindly note the playbook:

A/. Free samples and demonstrations at the worksite, not in a catalogue.

B/. The Fevicol Furniture Book, started in 1991 and now 31 volumes of designs that carpenters show their customers.

C/. Training programmes, and a loyalty network called the Fevicol Champions Club.

The most persuasive salesman Fevicol ever had doesn't work for Pidilite. He works for you, in your living room, with a pencil behind his ear.

Then there is the arithmetic. Glue is roughly one or two per cent of what a wardrobe costs. Save fifty rupees on glue and risk a door falling off in front of the client's mother-in-law? No carpenter with a reputation to protect takes that bet. Never. Ever. Good for Pidilite: hopeless for any rival trying to win on price.

## Then came the elephant

Once the product and the carpenter were in place, the ads arrived to make the brand loved.

Picture the 1990s. A handful of channels, one television, and the whole family in front of it for the cricket or the Sunday film. Not every home had cable. Print and roadside hoardings did the rest. With so few screens, an ad wasn't skipped. It was watched together, argued over at the paan shop and performed in the school corridor next morning. Brands couldn't buy attention with targeting. They had to earn it with ideas.

Ogilvy, with Piyush Pandey as creative director, gave Fevicol a voice that sounded nothing like an adhesive. In 1997 came a tug of war in which even an elephant can't move the rope.

॥ "Dum laga ke haisha." Heave, with all your might.

Then a hen whose egg refuses to crack. In 2002, an impossibly overloaded bus that stays upright, with a Fevicol poster on the back. That one won a Silver Lion at Cannes. In 2004, an overloaded boat for Fevicol Marine, steady as a Test opener on the last day.

॥ "Fevicol ka mazboot jod hai, tootega nahi." It's a Fevicol bond. It won't break.

![Tootega hi nahi. (© Sethi)](images/fevicol-bridge.jpg)

Notice what these films never do. No chemistry, no lab coat, no "strongest bond in its class". They take one promise, that it won't come apart, and stretch it until it becomes a joke. And a joke, unlike a specification, gets passed on. Outstanding.

When Pandey died in October 2025, India's tributes reached, almost by reflex, for his Fevicol lines. Few admen get a farewell written in their own copy.

![Adios, Fevicol ka jod. (Tribute: Thinkster)](images/piyush-pandey-tribute.jpg)

Fevikwik, the instant cousin, got its own legend in 1999. A sophisticated angler sits by a river with nothing to show for his patience. A villager dabs four drops on a stick, dips it in and pulls out four fish.

॥ "Chutki mein chipkaaye." Sticks in a snap.

Fevicol owned the bond that lasts. Fevikwik owned the fix that's quick. Two products, two promises, no confusion. When Pidilite bought M-Seal from the Mahindras in 2000, it repeated the trick: a darkly funny film in which one drop from a leaking pipe washes away a greedy son's doctored will. Keep M-Seal at home, always. Many of us first met these ads between cartoons and remember them better than the cartoons.

## When the brand becomes the word

Listen to how India actually shops.

A/. "Teen rupay ka Xerox kar do." Nobody photocopies anything.

B/. "Paanch rupay ka Cadbury la do." It's a chocolate, but only on the wrapper.

C/. "Das rupay ka Maggi." Instant noodles exist, apparently, in some other country.

And "Fevicol laga do". Nobody says adhesive, and nobody says glue. When a brand becomes the noun, the customer stops looking left or right. There is no comparison, because there is nothing to compare it with. The brand isn't on the shortlist: it is the list.

The surest sign? Designers and meme-makers now make Fevicol ads for free, unpaid and unasked, every Holi and every heartbreak. Some of them are pinned to this page. No agency fee, no media plan, and the brand still gets the last laugh.

![Kyun, nahi chauke na? Happy Holi, from the internet](images/fevicol-holi.jpg)

That is the closest thing to a monopoly that marketing can build without a licence from the government. Pidilite holds roughly 70 per cent of India's branded adhesives market, and it has kept Fevicol's name on the tip of the nation's tongue for more than six decades, in a business where every sector, from furniture to footwear to packaging, needs something to hold it together. Rivals have arrived with cheaper tubes and louder shelves. None has pricked the bubble. Take a bow, Pidilite.

But a pat on the back comes with a warning label. A brand name that turns into an everyday word can be lost to the dictionary. In America, "escalator" was once a trademark. Xerox spent years running ads begging people not to use its name as a verb. And there is a quieter danger closer home. When a brand becomes the category and goes silent, shoppers start accepting whatever the dealer hands them, because "it's all Fevicol anyway". That is how a leader loses its premium without losing its name... which explains why a sixty-seven-year-old brand still makes ads mocking cheap glue that doesn't hold. Fevicol isn't fighting a rival. It is fighting the habit of being taken for granted.

![The only broken thing it can't fix](images/fevicol-404.jpg)

Remember Dalda? It didn't lose to a better ad. It lost when people came to believe something else was better for them. Fevicol has never had its Dalda moment. Not yet, anyway.

## A survival strategy for Monday morning

If you're building a brand in a dull category, here is the Fevicol lesson in four lines.

Solve a pain people have stopped complaining about. Find the person who actually decides, even if he never pays. Make one promise and make it funny, because people repeat jokes, not specifications. And once your name becomes the word for the thing, keep talking. Silence is how a noun goes back to being a brand among many.

Ads made Fevicol famous. Carpenters made it the default. The language made it permanent.

Tootega nahi. Not even after sixty-seven years.
