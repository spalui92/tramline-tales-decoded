Kindly note: Jaguar's loudest car advertisement of 2024 contained no car.

No long bonnet. No leather. No road disappearing into the Scottish mist. No engine note making the neighbour reconsider his life choices.

Instead, there were models in extravagant clothes, saturated colours, phrases such as Delete Ordinary and Live Vivid, and a new Jaguar wordmark that looked as if a luxury fashion house had been asked to spell the name.

The car would come later.

The outrage did not.

On 18 November 2024, Jaguar unveiled a completely reimagined identity built around what it called Exuberant Modernism. The new system included a geometric wordmark, a horizontal strikethrough device, vivid primary colours, a new monogram and a redrawn version of the leaping cat. The leaper was not technically killed, despite several online obituaries. Jaguar kept it as what the company called a precious mark of provenance.

That distinction matters, because this was not a logo tidy-up. Jaguar itself called the exercise a transformation.

॥ "The cat needed to move. The question is whether it needed to change its spots."

## The Snapshot

What happened: Jaguar prepared for an all-electric, considerably more expensive future by attempting a near-total brand reset.

What it wanted: fewer cars, higher margins, a new generation of wealthy clients and a position above the crowded premium battlefield where it had spent years fighting much larger German rivals.

What the audience first saw: fashion, colour, art and a new visual language.

What it did not see was a Jaguar.

The verdict: the business case for changing Jaguar was stronger than the campaign made it look. The strategy had logic. The sequencing, in my view, had considerably less.

॥ "Jaguar needed reinvention. It did not need amnesia."

![Bold reinvention or branding misstep? The argument was already designed before the car arrived.](images/jaguar-rebrand-autopsy.jpg)

## First, the Cat Really Was in Trouble

Nostalgia is lovely. It is hopeless at paying salaries.

Jaguar retailed 174,560 vehicles in FY2017/18. By FY2023/24, that figure was 66,866. Different product cycles and market conditions sit behind those numbers, and Jaguar's sales had moved around in the years between, but the long arc is difficult to romanticise. The brand had become much smaller.

That matters because BMW, Mercedes-Benz and Audi could play a scale game Jaguar could not realistically win. They had broader line-ups, bigger volumes and the economics that follow both.

Put differently, people liked Jaguar rather more enthusiastically than they bought Jaguar.

There is a difference.

The company had already announced its broader Reimagine strategy before the colourful people appeared on everybody's timeline. Jaguar was heading towards an all-electric future and, more importantly, towards a smaller and more profitable position higher up the luxury ladder.

So let us retire one convenient argument immediately.

The 2024 rebrand was not born because somebody in marketing got bored on a Thursday.

There was a commercial problem underneath it.

![The XF years: old Jaguar grammar, stated without subtitles.](images/jaguar-xf-2008.jpg)

Jaguar managing director Rawdon Glover later explained the economics rather plainly. The company had spoken to about 1,000 prospective clients across Britain, North America, Europe and China. It used research including conjoint analysis to test what people might be prepared to pay. Glover said the new proposition had to look as though it was worth roughly £120,000.

Higher prices meant Jaguar would not need huge volumes. The whole ownership experience, from dealerships to service and hospitality, would also have to change because the proposed customer was not merely buying transport. Jaguar was asking for a very different willingness to pay.

There, underneath all the pink, is the actual management slide.

A/. Segment.

B/. Target.

C/. Position.

Jaguar was changing all three.

## The Old Jaguar Was Selling More Than Horsepower

This is the bit a spreadsheet can miss.

A premium car gets you somewhere.

A luxury car tells people something while you are getting there.

That something is partly engineering, partly design, partly scarcity and partly a bundle of associations accumulated over years. Luxury buyers do not all want those associations displayed at the same volume, but research on luxury consumption has shown that brand prominence itself can work as a status signal. Quiet luxury and loud luxury speak differently; both rely on the audience recognising the code.

Jaguar had codes.

The leaper. The long bonnet. The low stance. Britishness without having to print a Union Jack on the door. Grace before aggression. A sense that the car was expensive, but did not need to shout the invoice through a megaphone.

![Before Exuberant Modernism: the car wore the suit, and the suit knew its place.](images/jaguar-pre-rebrand-luxury.jpg)

Brand heritage can have commercial value too. Research in the Journal of Business Research has found that emphasising heritage can strengthen perceived quality and willingness to pay a premium. Another study on logo redesigns found that strongly committed customers can react more negatively to substantial visual changes than weakly committed customers.

Which, when you think about it, is hardly shocking.

The people most likely to notice that you moved the furniture are the people who knew where the furniture was.

This does not mean a legacy brand must preserve every badge in amber. That way lies the heritage museum.

It means you should know which pieces of the past are doing commercial work before you throw them into the skip.

![A chrome cat once did a remarkable amount of the talking.](images/jaguar-leaper-grille.jpg)

## Then They Put the PowerPoint in a Fashion Show

Jaguar took one of founder Sir William Lyons's best-known principles and made it the spine of the reset.

॥ "A Jaguar should be a copy of nothing."

Excellent line.

The problem is that the first execution seemed determined to prove the statement by copying almost nothing from Jaguar either.

The new identity was built around four symbols of change: the device, the strikethrough, the exuberant colour palette and the maker's marks, including the redesigned leaper. Then came the campaign film.

Brightly dressed people. Architectural spaces. Fashion-film choreography. Colour everywhere.

No car.

![The new grammar: people first, saturated colour and the 2024 wordmark taking centre stage.](images/jaguar-copy-nothing-campaign.jpg)

If the objective was to stop the scroll, congratulations. Please collect the trophy on your way out.

If the objective was to transfer decades of Jaguar associations into a new six-figure proposition, the question becomes considerably harder.

॥ "Attention and positioning are not the same thing. A fire alarm gets attention too."

This is the campaign's central tension.

Jaguar wanted to escape traditional automotive advertising. Fair enough. Another silver car carving through another mountain road would hardly qualify as Copy Nothing.

But differentiation is only useful when the audience still understands what has been differentiated.

Jaguar was asking people to process, almost simultaneously:

A/. A different logo.

B/. A different visual language.

C/. A different audience.

D/. A different powertrain strategy.

E/. A different price bracket.

F/. A different idea of what Jaguar itself meant.

That is a lot of luggage for one cat.

## The Psychology of Prestige

This is where my own discomfort with the campaign begins.

Jaguar, to me, has always represented more than expensive mobility. It carried a certain regalness. Reputation. Ownership as an event. The sense that you bought one partly because not everybody could.

That is subjective, of course, and it should not be confused with a universal customer truth. But luxury branding is built precisely from these subjective meanings repeated often enough to become recognisable assets.

The old Jaguar proposition could sometimes be compressed into one image: predator, machine, road.

![The old proposition in one frame: predator, machine, recognition.](images/jaguar-real-recognises-real.jpg)

You did not need a manifesto to understand the intended personality.

The 2024 campaign inverted that logic. Instead of starting with recognition and adding novelty, it started with novelty and asked recognition to catch up later.

There is a management argument for doing exactly that. Jaguar did not want its historical customer profile to dictate its future. Glover said the company wanted to attract new people, and the price architecture meant it could afford to sell far fewer cars. A polarising product can work when the business only needs a relatively small number of people to love it intensely.

That is perhaps the strongest defence of the project.

A £130,000 object does not need ten people saying, quite nice.

It needs one person saying, where do I sign?

## When the Internet Roared Back

The response was immediate enough to make Jaguar one of the most discussed car brands that week without actually showing anybody a car.

Some criticism was useful.

Some was predictable culture-war sludge prompted by the casting and styling of the advertisement. Jaguar's leadership condemned the more abusive and intolerant reaction, and rightly so. That material tells us very little about whether the positioning strategy works.

The useful criticism was simpler.

Where is the car?

Elon Musk compressed the category problem into four words when he asked Jaguar whether it sold cars.

Glover defended the approach. The campaign was deliberately designed to move away from automotive stereotypes, and he said it had generated more than 160 million views.

That is reach.

It is not yet purchase intent.

It is certainly not proof of failure either.

This is where campaign autopsies become lazy very quickly. Social-media mockery is not a sales report. Search volume is not brand equity. Views are not deposits. A meme can damage a campaign, amplify it, or simply make everybody temporarily aware that it exists.

So the honest answer in late 2024 was not Jaguar won or Jaguar lost.

The honest answer was: we have noticed the patient. Now show us the prognosis.

## What Jaguar Was Actually Selling

Here is the part that makes the whole campaign more intelligent than its critics sometimes allowed.

Jaguar was not really selling an EV.

It was selling permission to charge more.

॥ "The logo fight was the visible story. Willingness to pay was the business story."

The plan was to leave the volume-premium fight and occupy space further upmarket. That explains the art references. It explains Miami Art Week. It explains the interest in fashion, creators and cultural collaborations. It explains why Jaguar wanted its dealerships to feel less like rows of cars on carpet and more like a luxury experience.

Seen through that lens, the campaign was not random at all.

It was Jaguar trying to behave less like a car manufacturer and more like a luxury house.

![Underneath the logo fight sat the actual strategy: Jaguar goes all-electric.](images/jaguar-project-roar-electric.jpg)

And this is where the management student in me has to give the campaign credit.

The old position was not working well enough. A halfway rebrand might have produced the worst of both worlds: too familiar to attract a new audience, too changed to reassure the old one.

Jaguar therefore chose a side.

That is strategy.

The argument is over whether it chose to burn more bridges than the strategy required.

## Then the Car Turned Up

Two weeks after the identity launch, Jaguar revealed the Type 00 concept at Miami Art Week.

Now the colours had an object to sit on.

Long bonnet. Huge proportions. Twenty-three-inch wheels. A glassless rear. Miami Pink and London Blue. Brass, stone and textiles inside. The leaper appeared on the car as a mark of provenance.

Whatever one thinks of the styling, nobody could accuse the thing of looking as though a committee had benchmarked an Audi.

![Type 00, finally. The mood board got a bonnet.](images/jaguar-type00-prowl.jpg)

This is where Jaguar's defence becomes stronger.

Type 00 looked like Copy Nothing.

It was strange, theatrical, excessive and difficult to confuse with the increasingly anonymous aerodynamic shapes occupying the EV market.

Take a bow for the courage.

My problem is that I would have liked to meet the car before being asked to forget the Jaguar I already knew.

The sequence was backwards.

Brand first. Evidence later.

The campaign asked for faith before showing the congregation the miracle.

## What Jaguar Got Right

Credit first, because it is deserved.

A/. It recognised that the old middle ground was not working. Trying to become a smaller BMW forever would have been a strategy only in the sense that standing still on a railway line is a travel plan.

B/. It chose a position rather than a compromise. Jaguar decided that being mildly liked by everybody was less useful than being intensely wanted by somebody.

C/. The move was researched. Roughly 1,000 prospective-client conversations do not guarantee success, but they make the idea that the strategy was invented over lunch difficult to defend.

D/. The product eventually supported the theatre. Type 00 looked like the advertising. Nothing kills a dramatic rebrand faster than unveiling a beige crossover six months later.

E/. Jaguar made itself culturally visible again. For a brand whose deeper problem was commercial relevance, indifference would have been considerably more dangerous than memes.

All fair.

Now the invoice.

## What I Still Cannot Defend

First, the brand codes changed too quickly and too publicly before the product could carry them.

If you are moving a familiar brand into unfamiliar territory, visual continuity is not cowardice.

It is a bridge.

Second, the campaign underused the very heritage that helped justify the future premium.

This is the contradiction I cannot get past.

Jaguar based the transformation on a quotation from its founder, then produced an opening act that visually behaved as though the past were an awkward family photograph.

You cannot simultaneously say our founder's philosophy matters and treat recognisable inheritance as a creative inconvenience.

Third, the absence of the car became the story.

There is a clever version of withholding the product.

There is also a version where the audience spends the launch asking what you sell.

Mystery works best after recognition.

Fourth, the logo had too much work to do.

The new wordmark may be cleaner. It may be easier to use digitally. It may sit beautifully inside the new design system.

But luxury identity is not a Canva template.

The question is not whether the logo looks contemporary in isolation. The question is whether the total system carries forward enough accumulated meaning to justify the next price tag.

For me, it did not.

## Two Years Later, Kindly Check the Garage

We now have an advantage the November 2024 comment section did not.

There is finally a production car.

On 7 October 2026, Jaguar unveiled the Type 01 in New York. It is a luxury electric four-door GT, with production beginning in 2027 and UK prices starting at £130,000. Jaguar says it will offer up to 1,030PS, a 118kWh usable battery and an expected EPA range of up to 400 miles.

More importantly for this case file, the Type 01 proves that the 2024 campaign was not merely mood-board theatre.

The dramatic proportions survived.

The unusual visual language survived.

The all-electric strategy survived.

The higher price survived.

And then something very interesting happened.

Jaguar began talking loudly about lineage again.

The company says the Type 01 name references its legendary predecessors. The official launch describes the car as carrying clear lineage to great Jaguars of the past. The leaper appears on the exterior. British heritage is back in the company language.

Heritage came home.

Of course it did.

Luxury has a funny habit of rediscovering the family silver after the housewarming party.

There is also an important warning for anyone trying to settle the rebrand argument with sales numbers. Jaguar retailed 48,445 vehicles in FY2024/25, down 27.5 per cent year on year, but JLR explicitly attributed that decline to the planned wind-down of the old Jaguar range during the transition. Many legacy models had reached the end of production by December 2024.

That number therefore cannot honestly be presented as proof that people saw the pink advertisement and stopped buying Jaguars.

Likewise, Type 01 orders are expected to open only in early 2027.

Anyone declaring the rebrand a commercial triumph today is early.

Anyone declaring it a commercial disaster is early too.

The exam paper has finally been handed out.

Nobody has collected it.

## The Road I Would Have Taken

Suppose the brief landed on my desk.

Jaguar needs to leave the volume-premium fight.

Jaguar needs a six-figure electric future.

Jaguar needs younger clients.

Jaguar needs to look nothing like another German executive car.

Fine.

I would still change it.

Just differently.

![The older recipe: long bonnet, leaper, dark red, and no explanatory manifesto required.](images/jaguar-heritage-red-f-type.jpg)

A/. Keep the leaper in the driver's seat. Redraw it. Simplify it. Make it work at app-icon size. But do not make people hunt for it. The leaper is memory equity competitors would spend decades trying to build.

B/. Make Copy Nothing a bridge, not a bonfire. Start with Lyons's line, then prove that Jaguar has copied nothing before. Not with a sepia grandfather montage. Show the instinct: the proportions, the racing history, the E-Type, the nerve to make something slightly unreasonable. Then cut to the future. Same nerve. Different century.

C/. Put one inch of car in the first advertisement. Not the whole thing. A light. A wheel. A section of that absurd bonnet. Enough for the brain to say Jaguar, car, something new is coming. Then let the fashion people have the remaining twenty-seven seconds.

D/. Change the price and the experience together. If Jaguar intends to charge £120,000-plus, the repositioning should be felt in the showroom, service, materials, concierge experience, digital journey, launch events and owner access. To Jaguar's credit, this is close to what its management says it intends to do.

E/. Let art amplify the car, not replace it. Miami Art Week was an inspired venue after Type 00 existed. That is the order I would preserve. Product. Meaning. Culture. Not culture, confusion, product.

## My Hot Take

Did Jaguar need rebranding?

Yes.

I would be dishonest if I said otherwise.

The sales trajectory, scale disadvantage and move towards a smaller, higher-margin electric business made maintaining the old Jaguar indefinitely a far riskier decision than nostalgia allows us to admit.

Did Jaguar need this much rebranding?

That is where I get off the bus.

Jaguar represents, to me, a particular kind of prestige.

Not simply wealth.

Regalness.

The long bonnet. The animal poised to leap. The slightly unnecessary elegance of owning something that does more than you need because needing it was never the point.

A Jaguar is an asset partly because not everybody owns one.

That does not mean the future Jaguar needed walnut veneer, a V8 and a retired chairman driving through Surrey forever.

Modern luxury can be electric.

It can be minimalist.

It can wear pink.

It can show up at an art fair.

It can absolutely speak to a 35-year-old technology founder instead of a 65-year-old company chairman.

But it still needs consequence.

The kind of visual authority where the badge arrives before the explanation.

My problem with the 2024 campaign is therefore not that Jaguar changed.

॥ "For a brief moment, change became the product."

The strategy needed surgery.

The campaign treated recognition as tissue it could afford to cut away.

And the irony is magnificent. Jaguar went looking for a radically modern identity by returning to a sentence spoken by its founder.

Copy Nothing.

Precisely.

But remember something.

The best luxury brands do not drag their history behind them.

They turn it into a tailwind.

## The Monday Morning Takeaway

For anyone currently staring at a rebranding deck while somebody explains that the new sans-serif signals optimism, four questions.

A/. Are you changing because the market moved, or because the board got bored?

B/. Which visual asset would a customer recognise if you removed your name?

C/. What piece of your history helps justify the price you charge today?

D/. If the new campaign hid your logo, would people still know what business you are in?

Zero or one: you have a mood board.

Two or three: you probably have a rebrand.

Four: you may actually have a repositioning. Kindly call finance before calling Cannes.

Jaguar was right to leave the old road.

I am simply not convinced it needed to remove every familiar signpost on the way out.

The cat can learn new tricks.

Just leave the claws on.
