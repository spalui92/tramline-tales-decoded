There was a time when a family's standing was counted in horses. A good stable meant you had arrived. The horse was transport, of course, but it was also a statement: of money, of patience, of a household that knew how to keep a temperamental animal fed, shod and running.

Then the stable became a garage. And in a great many Indian homes, the animal parked inside it answered to one name.

Listen. You hear it before you see it. A slow, heavy beat, like a heart that refuses to hurry, coming up a Kolkata bylane at seven in the morning. Dogs look up. Uncles look up. Somebody's nephew stops mid-sentence. Thump. Thump. Thump. A Bullet does not arrive. It announces itself.

![Before the garage, the stable. The shadow still remembers.](images/re-horse-shadow.jpg)

Now, kindly note the strange part. The machine making that sound was British. It was born in a small Worcestershire town called Redditch, named after a government rifle factory and sold to the world with a slogan borrowed from the gunsmiths. Today it is built near Chennai and sold in more than 80 countries, and in 2026 it turned 125.

Somewhere in between, the British factory stopped and the Indian one did not.

This is not a story about India rescuing Britain. Nobody planned a takeover, and nobody outsmarted anybody. It is a quieter story, and a better one: a local partnership, set up to serve one demanding customer, that simply outlived its parent. India was not the hospital. India was the alluvial soil.

॥ "Made like a gun." Royal Enfield's old promise, and still the fairest description of a Bullet.

## A rifle factory's paperwork

Redditch was famous for needles and fishing hooks long before anyone there thought about motorcycles. In 1891, two businessmen, Bob Walker Smith and Albert Eadie, bought a local firm called George Townsend & Co. A couple of years later, the company won a contract to supply the Royal Small Arms Factory at Enfield, on the edge of London. The name stuck, crown and all. Royal Enfield was born out of a rifle factory's paperwork, and for decades it advertised itself in words a gunsmith might have chosen: Made Like a Gun.

![A century on one wall, and most of it began in Redditch.](images/re-archive-wall.jpg)

The first Royal Enfield motorcycle rolled out in 1901. That is the anniversary year, and it matters, because you will often see 1926 floating around the internet as the start of it all. It isn't. In 1926 a V-twin called the Model 180 sat in the line-up, often with a sidecar for the family, and a fire tore through the Redditch works. A busy year. Not a birthday.

The birthday that matters for this story is 1932, when Royal Enfield introduced a motorcycle called the Bullet. A single cylinder, a bit of swagger and a name that sounded like it was going somewhere in a hurry. Nobody in Redditch could have guessed that it would outlive the factory, the company and most of the men who built it.

Through the 1930s and 1940s, Royal Enfield was one of many British names on a crowded grid: BSA, Triumph, Norton, Ariel, Matchless. British motorcycles were, more or less, the world's motorcycles. They went everywhere the Union Jack had flown, and to plenty of places it hadn't.

One of those places was Madras.

## Enter the man at the counter

In 1949, two years after Independence, a businessman named K. R. Sundaram Iyer set up Madras Motors to import British motorcycles, Royal Enfields among them. Remember him. Business history loves the founder in the boardroom and forgets the dealer at the counter, but the dealer is usually the one who knows what the customer actually wants.

![Famous, English and on sale in New York. (Period US advertisement)](images/re-us-ad.jpg)

Madras Motors had everything Redditch did not. It knew Indian roads, which in 1949 often meant no road at all. It knew Indian buyers and Indian dealers. It knew which institutions bought vehicles by the hundred and what they complained about afterwards. Redditch had the engineering. Madras had the ground.

That same year, back in England, Royal Enfield launched a 350cc Bullet and a 500cc Twin. Two companies on two continents, and one product about to find its real home.

## The customer in olive green

Then came the order that changed the arithmetic.

Independent India had long borders to watch, many of them in places where a road was a rumour. In Kashmir and Rajasthan, the Army needed motorcycles for border patrol, and it needed them to be tough, simple and fixable by a soldier with a spanner and no spare-parts depot for three hundred kilometres.

![One cylinder, nothing to hide. A soldier could understand it, and fix it. (Photo: arrvenn)](images/re-engine-cutaway.jpg)

In 1952, it ordered 350cc Bullets. How many? Royal Enfield's current timeline says 500. An older annual report put the figure at 800. The archive, it seems, is still arguing with itself. Either way, it was a large order from the most demanding customer in the country, and more orders followed.

Here is the part that turns a sale into a strategy. An institutional buyer of that size does more than buy motorcycles. It turns a trickle of imports into an argument for building them locally. If the Army is going to keep needing Bullets, and keep needing spares, why ship every one of them halfway round the world?

As one popular account of the brand's history tells it, the government of the day was keen that the motorcycles be made in India, not merely sold here. Whatever the exact mix of policy and persuasion, the logic was plain. A committed buyer, a capable local partner and a product built for punishment. That is not charity. That is a business case.

॥ "Tough, simple, fixable." Three words that won the Army over, and later, everybody else.

## A factory in Tiruvottiyur

In 1955, the Redditch company and Madras Motors formed a new venture called Enfield India. Read the shareholding carefully, because this is where the story turns. According to Royal Enfield's own history, Madras Motors held the majority. Redditch supplied the design, the licence and the know-how. Madras supplied the market, the capital, the customer and the controlling stake.

![The speedometer has opinions. The rider ignores them.](images/re-el-clasico.jpg)

In 1956, a factory opened at Tiruvottiyur, on the northern edge of Madras. At first it did what such factories usually did: it assembled kits shipped from England, motorcycles arriving in boxes and leaving on wheels. In 1957 the tooling followed. Part by part, the components that once came from England began to be made in Madras. By the early 1960s, the factory was building the machines in-house.

Kindly note what had happened. Nobody had stolen anything. A licensed assembly line had quietly grown roots.

And the product suited the soil. The Bullet was not clever. It was robust. It could be put right by the roadside mechanic under the neem tree, who learnt its moods the way an old syce once learnt a horse's. In a country where the next service centre might be a day's ride away, simple was not a compromise. Simple was the feature.

## Two factories, two futures

Back in Redditch, the weather was turning, and not only the English kind.

The British motorcycle industry spent the 1960s being squeezed from every side. Japanese manufacturers arrived with motorcycles that were cheaper, cleaner and more reliable, and that did not leave a puddle of oil on the garage floor. British firms, many still run the old way, were slow to answer with competitive new models. Market share slipped, the economics soured, and one famous name after another began to wobble. This was not one company's blunder. It was an entire industry missing its turn.

![1965: "In step with modern Britain". Five years later, Redditch went quiet. (Advertisement: Enfield Cycle Co. Ltd)](images/re-olympic-1965.jpg)

Royal Enfield tried. Its mid-1960s advertising was all optimism, new towers and clean lines. But the range kept shrinking. By the start of 1970, the company's own timeline lists just two models left: the 250cc Continental GT and the 736cc Interceptor. The motorcycle business had already changed hands in the late 1960s. The Redditch works closed in 1970. The last Interceptors were finished in an underground facility at Upper Westwood, which shut in June that year. The company itself was dissolved in 1971.

Three events, kindly note, not one. A factory closing, production ending and a company being wound up are different things, and the Bullet's story sits in the gaps between them.

Meanwhile, in Madras, the Bullet was doing what it had always done. Thumping.

The difference was not talent, and it was not luck. The Indian business stood on different ground. It had one focused product with a loyal market, an institutional buyer, a local owner with real skin in the game and a factory that no longer depended on parts from England. When the parent caught a cold, the child no longer needed its medicine.

![The street in the tank, the tank in the street.](images/re-tank-reflection.jpg)

## 1977: the Bullet goes home

Now picture this.

Britain has gone quiet. The works at Redditch stand empty. The machine that once crossed the sea in a crate, a foreign product for a young country, has been made in Madras for two decades. Indian hands. Indian tools. Indian parts.

And in 1977, Enfield India starts shipping the 350cc Bullet to Britain and Europe.

Same name. Same thump. Opposite direction.

Read that again, slowly. A former colony's licensed workshop was now sending the former empire's motorcycle back to the empire's own roads. The horse had returned to the stable it once left, and found the stable empty.

॥ "The factory in Britain had gone quiet. The Bullet had not." The whole story, in two sentences.

## The lean years, and a second act

It would be lovely to end there, with a flag and a fanfare. Real life is less tidy.

The Indian business had hard decades of its own. Through the 1970s and 1980s it struggled, and the Bullet, once a machine for the Army and the well-off, risked becoming a machine for nostalgia and very little else. Enfield India even built a diesel Bullet at one point, the sort of idea that only makes sense in a country that has done the maths on fuel prices. Surviving, it turned out, was not the same as thriving.

![1995, says the photo. A year after the company in Madras became Royal Enfield Motors.](images/re-1995.jpg)

Enter Eicher, a company better known in those days for tractors and trucks. Read the dates carefully, because they are often squashed into one. Eicher took a 26 per cent stake in 1990. It moved to a 60 per cent majority in 1993. And in 1994, Enfield India changed its name to Royal Enfield Motors Ltd.

The new owners made the decision that, in hindsight, built the modern brand. Stop chasing every segment. Move away from small lightweight motorcycles and concentrate on the mid-size ones, the space between the commuter and the superbike, where the Bullet had always lived. One clear identity instead of five confused ones.

It is the oldest lesson in retail, and every shopkeeper in Burrabazar knows it. You cannot be the shop for everything. Be the shop for one thing, and be the first name people think of.

## Old is not the same as relevant

Here is the nuance the nostalgia posters tend to miss. Royal Enfield did not stay relevant simply because it was old. Old things die all the time. Ask the Ambassador. Heritage gave the brand distinctiveness. Relevance had to be earned, with money and with engineering.

![Old school, back in London. The loop closes. (Image: Royal Enfield)](images/re-bullet-650-london.jpg)

So the money came. Production spread beyond Tiruvottiyur to new plants at Oragadam and Vallam Vadagal, with modern lines and real capacity. The engineering came too. Around 2008 and 2009, a new-generation engine and the Classic range put modern internals inside the old silhouette. In 2016, the Himalayan took the brand off the tarmac and into adventure touring. From 2018, the 650 twins opened another chapter abroad.

And then something rather lovely happened. By the 2010s, the company was building a technical presence in Britain again, blending Chennai and British engineering under one roof. The story stopped being India versus Britain. It became a loop.

The brand also learnt to sell something you cannot bolt on: belonging. Group rides, owners' meets, touring events, jackets, helmets. Buying a Royal Enfield began to feel less like buying a vehicle and more like joining a club with a very loud membership card.

![The oldest motorcycle brand in continuous production, as the brand puts it. Continuous, thanks to Madras.](images/re-classic-350-white.jpg)

## 125 years on, in numbers

Now the arithmetic, with the dates attached, because a number without a date is only decoration.

A/. In FY2025-26, Royal Enfield sold 1,227,977 motorcycles, up 22 per cent on the year before: 1,107,343 in India and 120,634 abroad.

B/. In the 250 to 350cc segment, it held about 95 per cent of domestic sales between April and December 2025. In the 500 to 800cc band, about 97 per cent of dispatches. These are shares of its chosen segments, not of the whole market. But inside those segments, the competition is mostly a rounding error.

C/. By September 2026, it had more than 2,074 stores in India, about 1,200 more across 80-plus countries, and seven assembly facilities abroad, two of them in Brazil.

D/. Its One Ride, a day when owners ride out together, drew more than 150,000 riders in 60 countries in September 2026.

![From one importer's counter to more than 2,000 stores in India. Helmets sold separately.](images/re-showroom.jpg)

Put those beside the bigger picture. India bought 20.5 million two-wheelers in calendar 2025. Most were commuters: sensible, frugal machines built to get a clerk to the office and back on a litre and a prayer. A Royal Enfield is not that motorcycle. It costs more, it weighs more and it drinks more. Nobody buys one because it is the sensible choice.

Which is exactly the point. Over seven decades, the Bullet moved from duty to desire. The Army's workhorse became the college boy's dream, then the salaried man's weekend, then the woman rider's statement, then the retired uncle's second innings. Not cheap, but no longer exotic. Within reach, if you saved for it. And once it was within reach, people wanted it.

The 125th birthday arrived in 2026 with an anniversary Classic 650, limited to 125 units per region and priced at Rs 4,25,589 ex-showroom in India. A birthday cake with a waiting list.

The fans, meanwhile, have long since started doing the marketing themselves. Designers make Royal Enfield posters for fun. Photographers shoot tank badges in black and white as if they were portraits of old relatives. Nobody asked them. Nobody paid them. That is what heritage looks like when it is working.

![Fans now design the posters for free. Heritage, selling itself. (Design: Ravico)](images/re-ravico-poster.jpg)

## Why India was the alluvial soil

So why did the Bullet put down roots in Madras instead of withering with Redditch? Strip away the romance, and four things remain.

A/. A local partner with a stake. Madras Motors was not a distant agent collecting commission. It held the majority, so it had everything to lose.

B/. A demanding first customer. The Army's order gave the venture volume, credibility and a reason to build locally.

C/. A product that fit the ground. Simple, strong and fixable is a dull pitch in a showroom and a priceless one on a mountain road.

D/. Production close to the buyer. Once the tools and the parts were made in Madras, the Indian business no longer depended on the health of the British one.

None of this was a masterstroke drawn up in a boardroom. It was a run of sensible decisions, each made for its own reasons, that added up to something nobody had planned: a second home that turned out to be the permanent one.

![An old Indian doorway, a newer Bullet. The soil was ready long before the seed.](images/re-arch-bullet.jpg)

## A survival lesson for Monday morning

If you run a brand, or work for one, the Royal Enfield lesson fits in four lines.

Find the partner who knows the ground, and give them a real stake. Win the customer who is hardest to please, because everyone else follows. Build close to where you sell. And when your heritage starts to look like your strategy, invest anyway, because old is not the same as relevant.

The horse never left. It only changed its address.

Thump. Thump. Thump. Somewhere in a Kolkata bylane, an uncle looks up.
