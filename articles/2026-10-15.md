Kindly note: in August 2026, Liquid Death asked America to donate its urine to cool AI data centres.

Not literally. A disclaimer made sure nobody posted anything warm. But the film was real: Jason Kelce, a mascot called Murder Man, a music video, and a joke about how much water the machines drink while the rest of us are told to take shorter showers.

Seven weeks later, on 5 October, the same company walked onto the Tech Stage at Advertising Week New York and explained how it now runs its marketing on an AI operating system.

Hypocrisy? Not quite. It is something more interesting. The punk is on the can. The plumbing is in the back office. And the plumbing, it turns out, is where the money is.

॥ Mock the machine in public. Hire it in private.

## First, the legend

Liquid Death is water. In a can. Shaped like a tallboy of beer, dressed like a heavy-metal album and sold with a tagline that tells you to murder your thirst. As a product, it offers nothing new. That has always been the point.

![The product is water. The purchase is a personality.](images/ld-more-than-product.jpg)

The founder, Mike Cessario, a former Netflix creative director who had played in bands, got the idea at a Warped Tour, where musicians were drinking water out of energy-drink cans so they would look the part on stage. Energy drinks had been selling menace for years. Cessario simply poured water into the costume.

![The swagger it borrowed. (Photo: Yamamichi Studio)](images/ld-monster-closeup.jpg)

He filed the trademark in July 2017. The water itself reached customers in January 2019.

Before there was a single can to sell, he spent about $1,500 on a deliberately absurd video in which a marketing executive is "waterboarded" with the product. It went viral. Investors noticed. A brand had been born before the bottle, sorry, the can, existed.

From there, the arithmetic of hype: $263 million in retail sales in 2023, and a $1.4 billion valuation in March 2024. Unicorn, with a skull on it.

![Shouldn't work. Did. (Poster: BRN)](images/ld-shouldnt-work.jpg)

Remember that $1,500. It comes back.

## The machine, explained without the brochure

Liquid Death's media is now run with Power Digital, a San Diego agency that in August 2026 unveiled what it calls an AI-native growth model. Strip away the press release and there are three parts:

A/. Iris, the brain. Three years in the making, trained on the agency's own playbook, thousands of incrementality tests and billions of dollars of transaction data. It reads the signals and recommends what to do.

B/. Omega, the hands. Not a dashboard. A shared cockpit where the agency's strategists, their AI agents and the client run campaigns together, with every recommended action logged against a KPI so somebody can later ask whether it worked.

C/. Creative Affinity, the critic. It studies creative assets to predict which ads will perform. It has existed since 2024, first built with help from Meta's engineers, and has now been folded into the ecosystem.

![Three cans, one tagline, no new ingredient. (Carousel: 1605 & Co)](images/ld-made-water-interesting.jpg)

Power Digital's CEO, Jeff Mason, offered a neat three-stage ladder. Most brands, he said, are AI-curious: everyone has ChatGPT or Claude open in a tab. Some are AI-assisted: they have built their own intelligence layer. A few are AI-native: work actually happens inside a custom application sitting on top of that layer. Liquid Death, naturally, was presented as the third kind.

## What actually changed

Here is the honest list, from the people who were on stage.

A/. The rhythm. Retail-media campaigns that used to be reviewed weekly, or twice a week, are now optimised daily.

B/. The questions. Instead of pinging the team on Slack or writing his own queries, the chief media officer, Benoit Vatere, goes into the system, gets the picture and shares it.

C/. The headcount ambition. Vatere's stated goal is to run a sizeable budget with two people internally.

D/. The software itself. Liquid Death produced 17 versions of its Omega environment in three weeks.

And the line that will be printed on many a startup's office wall this year:

॥ "I don't want to see any spreadsheets anymore."

His rule is simple. If a task can be automated, or takes more than five minutes, do it once to build the automation, and never by hand again.

## Monday morning, two buildings

Now picture two Mondays. Imagined, yes. But anyone who has worked inside a large company will recognise the first one in their bones.

At the giant:

9:00. The agency's weekly report lands. Sixty-four slides.

11:30. The brand team has reached slide nine.

2:00. A meeting to schedule the meeting about the report.

Thursday. Someone senior approves moving budget from one retailer to another.

Friday. The weekend sale has already happened. Without them.

At the can with the skull:

9:00. The system has already read last night's numbers.

9:05. Recommendations arrive, each one tied to a KPI.

9:20. A human approves two and kills one.

10:00. The bids have moved.

10:01. Chai.

![Loud wall, quiet machine. Decision made by 9:20.](images/ld-graffiti-can.jpg)

That, stripped of the stage lights, is the entire competitive argument made at Advertising Week. Not more money. Fewer corridors. Mason put it plainly: brands stuck at the AI-curious stage will not keep up with brands like Liquid Death.

A giant can outspend a challenger a hundred times over. It cannot out-decide it if every decision needs four signatures and a fortnight. Size used to be the moat. Now it is the traffic jam.

And Liquid Death has always known this game. It became famous on a $1,500 video because it moved before anyone could tell it not to. The AI does not change that instinct. It industrialises it.

॥ The giants have bigger budgets. The rebel has shorter corridors.

## The learning phase didn't die. It moved.

Anyone who has run a PPC campaign knows the learning phase: those first days when the algorithm burns money finding out who your customer is, and you must sit on your hands and not panic.

The fashionable reading of the Liquid Death story is that AI has abolished that phase. Feed the brain, push the button, be clever from day one.

Look again at that number: 17 iterations in three weeks.

That is the learning phase. It did not vanish. It moved from the ad account to the operating system. Instead of the algorithm learning the market while the brand watches, the brand and its agency are now teaching the machine how they think, version after version, almost daily. The tuition is still being paid. It is just paid in iterations rather than in wasted clicks.

And daily optimisation carries its own trap. A team that looks every morning sees every wobble. Some wobbles are signals. Most are weather. React to the weather and you will be very busy and slightly worse off. Speed is a virtue only when you know which decisions deserve it.

॥ Looking daily is easy. Knowing what not to touch is the job.

## Why the punk brand, of all brands?

Because its whole business was built on a problem the machine happens to be good at.

Liquid Death sells a product with no functional difference. It cannot win on what is in the can. It wins on attention, and on converting attention into a sale on a shelf or a screen. Vatere has said for a while that consumers are simply not loyal to packaged-goods brands, which is why he cares more about light and lapsed buyers than about the brand's smaller army of devoted fans.

![Mango Chainsaw. Somebody approved that name, and bless them.](images/ld-mango-chainsaw.jpg)

Which brings us to the most human moment of the panel. It took place the day before Amazon's Prime Day. The moderator mentioned that her Amazon basket already held some Liquid Death, waiting for the discount. Vatere was not delighted. Fans who stock up at the sale price, he explained, are customers who would have paid full price anyway. Every discounted can sold to them is money handed back. What he wants from Prime Day is new drinkers who have never tried the brand.

![Stocked, chilled and bought on discount. Vatere would rather you didn't.](images/ld-cooler.jpg)

So the most sophisticated AI stack in the room was, in the end, serving the oldest instruction in retail: please pay full price, and please bring a friend.

Last year, a trade profile of Vatere summed up his stance in five words: AI for plumbing, not punchlines. That is still the deal. The machine decides where the ads go. Humans still decide what is funny. The urine film was not written by Iris. Thank God.

![Thirst, murdered on sight.](images/ld-desert-thirst.jpg)

## Who owns the brain?

Here is the grey area nobody on stage dwelt on.

Mason's own advice was that frontier models no longer give anyone an edge, because everybody uses the same ones. The edge, he said, is your own centralised data and your own intelligence layer, which grows into a moat over time.

Fair. But whose moat is it?

Iris belongs to Power Digital. It was trained on Power Digital's tests and Power Digital's transaction data, and every client's experience presumably makes it a little cleverer. Liquid Death has spent three weeks and 17 versions teaching Omega how Liquid Death thinks. If the relationship ends one day, as agency relationships tend to, what leaves with the agency? The spreadsheets are gone. The knowledge lives in someone else's software.

![When the agency walks out, what's left in the smoke?](images/ld-smoke.jpg)

In the old world, when you fired the agency, you kept the reports. In the new one, the agency might keep the brain.

## So, a revolution?

Not yet. An acceleration, certainly.

What has genuinely changed is the agency's job description. The monthly deck is dying. The client no longer sits in the audience for a report; it sits in the cockpit as co-pilot. For agencies, that is a threat and an invitation in equal measure. For a small, fast brand, it is a way to punch above its headcount against companies with ten times the staff and a hundred times the meetings.

What has not changed: the product still needs a reason to exist, the ad still needs an idea, and the customer still needs a reason to pay full price.

![Menace, sold by the can since long before water joined in. (Monster Energy poster)](images/ld-monster-poster.jpg)

Liquid Death was built on a $1,500 video and a name that sounded like a dare. The AI did not create that. It cannot. What it can do is make sure that, once the joke lands, not a rupee, sorry, not a dollar, is wasted carrying it to the right person.

The machine runs the tramline. Somebody still has to give people a reason to get on.
