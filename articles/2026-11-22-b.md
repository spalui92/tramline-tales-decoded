## Act I · The Snapshot: A Pug, a Network and a Rather Expensive Bill

Somewhere in India in 2003, a small boy walked across a rickety wooden bridge, and a pug trotted faithfully behind him. Nobody on screen made a phone call. Nobody mentioned a tower, a tariff or a signal bar. And yet an entire country understood exactly what was being sold.

![Wherever you go, our network follows. A boy, a pug, a plank bridge and not one phone in sight. (Print: Hutch)](images/hutch-pug-bridge.png)

That is the deceptively simple part of this story. The expensive part came later, and it came in units that no advertisement could ever cover: spectrum auctions, licence fees, tower rentals, interest payments and a Supreme Court judgment that rewrote what "revenue" meant.

॥ "Wherever you go, our network follows." The promise that started it all.

This piece follows one brand line across three names. It begins with Hutch and its pug, moves through Vodafone's red takeover and the ZooZoos, folds in Idea Cellular and its "Sirji", runs straight into Reliance Jio and ends with Vi, the company that inherited every one of those promises along with every one of those bills.

The question underneath it all is a simple one. What happens when a brand promise outlives the business conditions that made it possible?

The one-line verdict, before the long walk: the pug did its job beautifully. The business behind it had a harder job, and the market made it harder still.

## Before the Pug: India's Telecom Battleground

To understand why a dog could become a network's best salesman, it helps to remember what Indian mobile telephony looked like in the 1990s.

Hutchison Max Telecom, a joint venture between Hong Kong's Hutchison Whampoa and India's Max Group, was set up in February 1992. In November 1994, it won the licence to run a mobile network in Bombay, and the service went out under a name that sounded like a perfume: Max Touch.

Mobile phones then were the size of a brick and the price of a scooter. In those early years, incoming calls cost money too; India's move to the calling-party-pays system around the turn of the millennium came only after its share of legal wrangling. A mobile phone was a status object, not a habit.

The map was regional. Licences were handed out circle by circle, and each circle had its own cast of operators: Airtel, BPL, Escotel, Birla's own venture and the state-owned networks that would later become BSNL and MTNL. Hutchison grew the way most did then, by entering one circle at a time. Delhi came in December 1999, Kolkata in July 2000 and Gujarat that September.

This produced a curious branding problem. In Mumbai, the network ran under the Orange name, licensed from France Telecom at a royalty. In most other circles, it answered to Hutch. Same company, different names, different colours, depending on which city you happened to be standing in.

It is worth keeping the company and the brand apart here. The company had been in India since 1992. The brand most Indians remember came later, and took a few years to settle into the single national identity that the pug would eventually carry.

## Finding Its Own Colour: The Hutch Positioning Strategy

Fragmented names are an expensive habit. Every regional identity needs its own advertising, its own signage and its own recall. By November 2005, Hutchison Essar had decided to fold Orange and the recently acquired BPL Mobile into a single Hutch brand, so that one name, one rate card and one service promise could run across all its circles. The orange tristars on the logo turned a bright pink.

Look closely at the two print ads in this piece and you can see that transition. The barbershop ad carries the older orange tristars. The bridge ad carries the pink one. The pug, sensibly, never changed.

![Two chairs, one haircut, one loyal customer. The older orange tristars sit top right. (Print: Hutch)](images/hutch-pug-barber.webp)

What did Hutch actually stand for? Strip away the colour and the positioning territory was narrow and smart: dependable connectivity that stays with you, personally. It was not "the fastest network" or "the cheapest calls". It was a network that follows the customer, quietly, without fuss.

Rivals were busy with other claims. Airtel built a premium, aspirational image around its music and its celebrities. The state networks leaned on reach. Price players leaned on price. Hutch chose companionship, which nobody else had claimed, and which happened to be very hard to argue with.

One distinction matters for everything that follows. A communication strategy is a promise about the network. It is not the network. Hutch could make the promise feel warm. Only towers, spectrum and engineering could make it true. That gap is the thread running through this entire piece.

## The Brief: Selling Something Nobody Can See

Marketing a mobile network is a strange job. There is no product to hold. Nobody opens a box of "coverage". You experience a network mostly when it fails: a dropped call in a lift, a blank screen in a basement, a voice that turns into a robot halfway through a sentence.

So how do you advertise reliability? Most telecom advertising of the era answered with technology: towers on hilltops, maps lit up with dots, men in suits on phones in improbable places. The trouble is that every operator could shoot the same tower, and every map looked alike.

The brief Ogilvy & Mather Mumbai worked on asked for something different: make an invisible service feel like a relationship. The answer, credited to the agency's Rajiv Rao and Mahesh V., and directed by Prakash Varma of Nirvana Films, was to drop the machinery altogether and show loyalty instead.

A recurring visual metaphor has a practical advantage that is easy to underrate. Once the audience learns it, every new advertisement needs only a second or two to land. The pug did not need to explain the network each time. The pug was the explanation.

## Campaign One: The Pug Takes the Lead

The 2003 campaign was part of Hutch's "You and I" platform, and its film did almost nothing, which was precisely the point. A boy walks. A pug follows. The boy goes up a slide, through a lane, across a bridge. The pug follows. A gentle song plays underneath. The line arrives at the end: wherever you go, our network follows.

The dog's name was Cheeka, a pug owned by a couple in Goa. The pug became, almost overnight, the most recognisable animal in Indian advertising. Storyboard18 records that Cheeka turned into the "Hutch dog" and the wallpaper Hutch customers downloaded most, and that pugs became noticeably more popular as household pets in the years that followed.

|> "...falling in love with it for the nth time." Sneha Yadav, Social Samosa, recalling the Hutch pug ad from her school days

The emotional logic was tidy. Loyalty and proximity stood in for reach. A dog that never leaves your side is a better image of coverage than any tower, because it carries feeling as well as information. The campaign stretched across television, print and outdoor, and the pug kept appearing in executions for years, even through the change of name that was coming.

It also left a lesson that later Vodafone executives would admit openly: a symbol this strong is a gift and a burden. When the brand needed to say something new, the pug was always sitting there, waiting to be asked along.

## The Pug Scoreboard: Did the Campaign Deliver?

Here is where a careful teardown has to slow down.

On creative fame, the evidence is clear. The film is still cited in India's advertising trade as a reference for how to sell an intangible. Ad veterans such as Piyush Pandey and Alyque Padamsee praised it. The wallpaper downloads, the pug craze and two decades of nostalgic articles all point the same way. Recall was enormous.

On commercial impact, the honest answer is more modest. Hutch grew strongly in these years. By the end of December 2006, Hutchison Essar had 23.3 million customers and a 16.4 per cent national market share, according to the figures Vodafone published when it agreed to buy the company.

But Indian mobile as a whole was exploding at the time. Phones were getting cheaper, tariffs were falling and new circles were opening. Hutch was also buying businesses, including BPL Mobile, and expanding its footprint. Subscriber growth in that environment cannot be credited to a dog alone. The campaign almost certainly helped people choose Hutch over a rival. It cannot be shown to have caused that choice in any measurable share.

The fair conclusion: the pug built preference and memory at an exceptional level. Whether that memory converted into revenue is a question the public data cannot answer on its own, and this piece will not pretend that it can.

## The Red Transition: Hutch Becomes Vodafone (2007)

On 11 February 2007, Vodafone agreed to pay US$11.1 billion in cash for the companies holding Hutchison Telecom International's 67 per cent stake in Hutchison Essar, implying an enterprise value of about US$18.8 billion. Essar retained the remaining stake. The deal closed in May, and the company became Vodafone Essar.

|> "An impressive, well run company that will fit well into the Vodafone Group." Arun Sarin, Vodafone CEO, on Hutchison Essar, February 2007

Then came the hard part: changing a beloved name without losing the love. On 21 September 2007, Hutch became Vodafone. Social Samosa reported that roughly Rs 250 crore was set aside for the transition marketing.

![Same pug, new colour. The blanket did most of the explaining. (Advertisement: Vodafone)](images/hutch-is-now-vodafone.jpg)

Ogilvy's answer was to let the one constant carry the change. In one film, the pug returns home to find the house painted red, hesitates, and then walks in. In others it curls up on a red mat or peeks out from under a red blanket. The message was spelled out in the line "Hutch is now Vodafone", with "Change is good" doing the reassuring.

॥ "Hutch is now Vodafone." A change of name, carried by a creature that never changed.

Within weeks, the new catchphrase settled in: "Make the most of now". It was attached to short films about the operator's alert services, and it signalled a brand that wanted to sound a little more energetic than the quiet, understated Hutch.

The transition was widely praised for its speed and smoothness, but the pundits were not unanimous. An afaqs piece from 2007 captured both sides. Brand consultant Harish Bijoor argued that the pug works best as a subdued presence rather than a superhero, and noted that the network itself sometimes failed to keep the promise. Another expert summed up the culture clash in a single phrase.

|> "Hutch was a latent brand, and Vodafone is a blatant one." Kapoor of Samsika, quoted by afaqs, 2007

Keep three milestones separate here. The acquisition was a financial event in February and May 2007. The legal entity became Vodafone Essar. The consumer brand changed in September. The pug bridged all three.

## Campaign Two: Enter the ZooZoos (2009)

In 2008, Vodafone settled on "Happy to help" as its service positioning. A pug raced across town with a little girl's forgotten ballet shoes, and the stores were rebranded as places where help was at your doorstep. Helpful, warm and very much in the pug's tradition.

॥ "Happy to help." A service promise wearing a puppy's face.

Then, in April 2009, the Indian Premier League's second season moved to South Africa because of the general election at home, and Vodafone moved its next idea there too. During the commercial breaks appeared a set of small, egg-headed, white creatures with dot eyes and balloon bellies, speaking in gibberish. India called them ZooZoos.

![Three ZooZoos, one microphone, zero words that anyone could translate. (Advertisement: Vodafone)](images/zoozoo-mic.jpg)

They looked animated. They were not. Ogilvy's team led by Rajiv Rao created them, Prakash Varma's Nirvana Films shot them in Cape Town, and they were played by real people inside body suits against painted, spray-shadowed sets. Each short film carried one value-added service: a missed-call alert, a cricket score, a caller tune. Around thirty commercials ran across that IPL season.

The visual language was distinctive enough to be recognised from across a room. Flat grey world. White bodies. Two dots and a mouth. No words. Every film was a tiny comedy with a product hidden in the punchline.

|> "The Zoozoos campaign resonated strongly with viewers in creating an emotional connect." Kumar Ramanathan, Chief Marketing Officer, Vodafone Essar, November 2009

## The ZooZoo Scoreboard: Popularity Versus Performance

The ZooZoos were a cultural event. The ZooZoo fan page, launched the same year, grew at a speed that Indian brands had rarely seen; afaqs later reported that it passed ten million fans, and Campaign India noted the official page had over 19 million likes by 2019. Ogilvy India took an Integrated Grand Prix and a Film Craft award at Goafest 2010. Vodafone even won PETA India's GlitterBox award for using humane alternatives to real animals.

![Cricket ice cream, the only flavour available during an IPL season. (Advertisement: Vodafone)](images/zoozoo-icecream.jpg)

Viewers remembered them with real affection, and still do.

|> "Vodafone had done this brilliantly with the ZooZoo ads." Kanak Arora, who grew up watching them, to ThePrint

|> "ZooZoo ads remain my favourite TVCs till date." Nisha Singh, ScoopWhoop

ThePrint went as far as to argue that, for many Indians, the ZooZoos are what they remember of the 2009 IPL season, more than the cricket.

Now the discipline. Popularity tells us the films were watched, enjoyed and shared. It does not tell us how many people activated the services being advertised, how many stayed with Vodafone because of them, or whether any revenue line moved. Brand metrics and business metrics are two different ledgers, and the public record only fills one of them for the ZooZoos.

![Bowled, by the look on his face. Every ZooZoo film carried one service inside one small joke. (Advertisement: Vodafone)](images/zoozoo-cricket.webp)

The characters came back several times: Super ZooZoos for 3G in 2011, a dancing Zumi "internet army" in 2013, a customer-experience series in 2014, Ninja ZooZoos for digital help in 2018 and a lockdown safety series in 2020. Each return was warmly received. By 2016, though, creative critics were already asking whether the magic could be recreated, and by 2019 the characters had quietly gone off air.

## The Advertising Face-Off: Pug Versus ZooZoos

It is tempting to crown a winner here. It would also be unfair.

The pug was a single, repeatable metaphor. It did one thing, loyalty equals reach, and did it with almost no effort. Its strength was clarity. Its weakness was range: a dog following a child cannot easily explain a caller tune or a data pack.

The ZooZoos were a system. A flexible cast in a fixed world could carry any message, one film at a time. Their strength was range and entertainment. Their weakness was that the characters could become more famous than whatever they were selling.

![The whole troupe on one page: ice creams, a red postbox, a cricketer and a proposal. (Collage: Vodafone ZooZoo advertisements)](images/zoozoo-collage.jpg)

Measured against five tests, the pattern is clear. On emotional attachment, the pug wins. On memorability, it is close to a draw. On message clarity for one core idea, the pug again. On flexibility, the ZooZoos, comfortably. On longevity, the pug, which survived from Hutch through 4G.

The campaigns served different jobs. Hutch needed one idea that made it feel reliable. Vodafone needed a way to talk about dozens of services without boring anyone. Both briefs were answered well.

## Act II · Idea Cellular: The Other Half of the Equation

While Vodafone was busy being red, another operator was busy being yellow.

Idea Cellular began in 1995 as Birla Communications, with GSM licences for Gujarat and Maharashtra. AT&T joined the next year, Tata Cellular merged in by 2000, and in 2002 the company took the name Idea. After its partners exited, the Aditya Birla Group became its sole owner, and the company listed in 2007.

Idea's strength was a strong footprint outside the big metros. It was built circle by circle, especially in western, central and southern India, with a reputation in smaller towns and semi-urban markets. Kumar Mangalam Birla would later describe the fit neatly: Vodafone was dominant in the metros, Idea in urban and semi-urban India.

![To the hundred million who chose Idea. A thank-you note set in the loudest typeface in the building. (Print: Idea Cellular)](images/idea-sirji-100-million.png)

Idea's advertising was every bit as distinctive as Vodafone's. In 2006, Lowe Lintas gave it the line "An idea can change your life". In 2007 came "What an Idea, Sirji!", with Abhishek Bachchan as a man who solved social problems with a mobile phone. In the first film, a village caught in caste violence is calmed when everyone is identified by phone number instead of surname. Later films took on literacy, democracy and the waste of paper.

॥ "What an Idea, Sirji!" A brand name that turned into a compliment, and then into a catchphrase.

The genius of the line was that it put the brand's own name into everyday speech. Every time someone said "what an idea" over chai, the company got a free mention. Very few brand names can be used as a compliment. Idea could.

![A tribute poster in the Sirji mould, not an Idea original: a social problem, a phone, and a solution. (Illustration)](images/idea-sirji-billboard.jpg)

It is important not to write Idea's history backwards. Before 2016, it was a substantial, growing, profitable operator with a large customer base and real spectrum in its key circles. Its vulnerabilities were the same as every Indian telco's at the time: heavy capital needs, debt taken on to buy spectrum, and thin margins in a crowded market. It was not a failing company. It was a strong regional-national player about to meet a market that changed its rules.

## The Merger: Two Giants Seek Greater Ground (2017 to 2018)

On 20 March 2017, Vodafone and the Aditya Birla Group announced that Vodafone India (excluding its stake in Indus Towers) would merge with Idea Cellular. Vodafone would initially hold 45.1 per cent. The Birla group would hold 26 per cent after buying a further 4.9 per cent from Vodafone for Rs 3,900 crore, with a path to equal ownership over time. Kumar Mangalam Birla would be chairman.

![The deal in one page: the stakes, the options and the two men who signed it. (Infographic, 2017 press coverage)](images/vodafone-idea-deal-anatomy.webp)

The two men at the table each found a memorable way to sell it.

|> "India is a bigger jewel now." Vittorio Colao, Vodafone Group CEO, to The Hindu, March 2017

|> "Idea is strong where Vodafone is weaker, Vodafone is strong where Idea is weaker." Vittorio Colao, at the Mumbai announcement, as reported by Reuters

|> "This is an idea whose time has come." Kumar Mangalam Birla, Aditya Birla Group Chairman, to ET Now, March 2017

The strategic rationale was straightforward and, at the time, persuasive. Scale: the combination would become India's largest operator. Spectrum: pooled airwaves in more circles. Footprint: metros plus small towns. Cost: one network instead of two in overlapping circles. And defence: a bigger company to face a well-funded new rival.

The deal closed on 31 August 2018, creating an operator with nearly 408 million customers and, by Vodafone's own figures, a revenue market share of 32.2 per cent. The companies had projected run-rate cost and capital expenditure synergies of Rs 14,000 crore a year by the fourth full year after completion, of which about 60 per cent, or Rs 8,400 crore, was expected from operating costs.

Note the wording: projected, run-rate, by the fourth year. That is a forecast, not a promise kept. Merger synergies are realised only when two networks, two billing systems, two distribution channels and two workforces actually become one, and that is slow, messy work.

![Red on top, yellow below. Two logos, one balance sheet. (Brand identities: Vodafone and Idea)](images/vodafone-idea-logos.jpg)

It would be lazy to call this two failing companies holding hands. Both brought assets: customers, spectrum, towers, distribution and two of the most loved brand stories in Indian advertising. The weakness was not in either company alone. It was in the timing, and in what the market was about to demand of them.

## Know Thy Competitor: What Airtel and Jio Were Doing Differently

A telecom story told only through Jio becomes a morality play. It is more useful to look at both of the merged company's main rivals.

Jio started with a clean sheet. It built an all-4G network from scratch, with no older 2G or 3G equipment to maintain, and used the deep balance sheet of Reliance Industries to fund it. It priced aggressively, pushed its own phones and built an ecosystem of apps around its connection.

Airtel had the same legacy burden as Vodafone and Idea, with older networks and older customers. What it did differently was keep investing through the downturn, defend its higher-paying customers, raise capital repeatedly and protect its average revenue per user. It also had other businesses, including Africa and enterprise, to lean on.

![Three columns, one wave: subscribers, spectrum and revenue share, laid side by side at the time of the merger. (Infographic, 2017 press coverage)](images/vodafone-idea-numbers.jpg)

Vodafone Idea, meanwhile, had to integrate two networks while fighting a price war, with less capital to spare than either rival. Choices that look obvious in hindsight, such as investing faster in 4G capacity, were constrained by a balance sheet already carrying spectrum debt.

The useful lens is not who was good and who was bad. It is who had the money, the network and the time to keep their promises when the market changed. In 2017, Vodafone Idea had the customers. It was shorter on the other three.

## Then the Ground Moved: Jio Changes the Rules (2016 Onwards)

On 5 September 2016, Reliance Jio launched commercially with free domestic voice calls and an introductory offer of free data. Within 170 days it had crossed 100 million subscribers.

For consumers, this was genuinely transformative. Data that had been a metered luxury became abundant. Hundreds of millions of Indians came online through cheap smartphones and cheaper data. Video, payments, maps and messaging became everyday habits rather than urban privileges. That benefit is real and should not be waved away.

For operators, the economics broke. The industry had earned its money on voice. Jio made voice free and data cheap, and incumbents had to match it. Average revenue per user across the industry fell sharply. Idea posted its first quarterly loss since listing in February 2017, and by early 2018 it had lost its place as the third-largest operator by revenue to Jio. The number of private operators shrank through exits and mergers until, in Airtel's words around the time of the 2019 AGR verdict, only two of the fifteen older private operators remained in business.

![SuperNet 4G, drummed in by a boy and his pug. The network promise, updated for the data age. (Advertisement: Vodafone)](images/vodafone-supernet-drummer.jpg)

Vodafone's own marketing in these years tells the story through its taglines. The pug returned in 2016 to launch SuperNet 4G, and Ogilvy's creative head admitted the difficulty of the job.

|> "Refreshing icons is always difficult." Kiran Anthony, Executive Creative Director, Ogilvy Mumbai, to exchange4media

In November 2017, Vodafone India replaced its line "Power to you" with "The future is exciting. Ready?", a brighter, more urgent promise pointed at a data future. It was a sensible line. It was also a promise that depended on 4G capacity, which depended on capital.

Jio alone does not explain everything that followed. A price war hurts everyone in it. The companies that survived it in better shape were the ones with the deepest pockets, the cleanest networks or both. That is the bridge to what lies underneath.

## Beneath the Network: The Six Structural Pressure Points

A brand can be in great shape while the business under it strains. For Vodafone Idea, six pressures compounded.

A/. Network infrastructure. Coverage and capacity are never finished. Every new phone, every new video habit and every new technology generation demands fresh towers, fibre and equipment. A network that stops investing does not stay the same; it falls behind.

B/. Spectrum and capital allocation. Spectrum is bought at auction, often paid for in deferred instalments, and its cost sits on the balance sheet for years. Buying too little hurts quality; buying too much drains cash.

C/. Operating costs. Two merged companies carried two sets of costs for some time. Squeezing out the duplication was essential, and it takes time.

D/. Debt and leverage. Deferred spectrum payments, interest and statutory dues piled up, leaving little room to borrow for new investment.

E/. Merger integration. Combining two networks, systems, sales channels and workforces is among the hardest jobs in business, and it was done during a price war.

F/. Customer experience and retention. Subscribers left in large numbers. It would be wrong to assume, from losses alone, that service was poor everywhere; churn also reflects pricing, rival offers and network coverage in specific regions. But customers who leave rarely come back cheaply.

![The Kochi 4G launch: proof that the promise kept being renewed, circle by circle. (Photo: Vodafone India 4G launch, Kochi)](images/vodafone-4g-kochi.jpg)

Jio was an external shock. These six were the internal vulnerabilities that determined how hard the shock landed. The two interacted. A balance sheet that could not fund 4G quickly meant weaker coverage in some areas, which made it easier for customers to leave, which reduced revenue, which made the balance sheet weaker still.

## Act III · The Regulatory Reckoning: AGR, Spectrum and the Cost of Staying Connected

To follow what happened next, two institutions need to be kept apart. The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India regulates the sector and recommends policy. The Department of Telecommunications issues licences and collects the government's share of operators' revenue. They are not interchangeable, and they are not perpetually at war.

Operators pay licence fees and spectrum usage charges as a percentage of their Adjusted Gross Revenue, or AGR. For years, operators argued that AGR should include only revenue from telecom services. The DoT argued it should include almost everything they earned, from interest income to asset sales.

On 24 October 2019, the Supreme Court sided with the DoT. The ruling ended a legal dispute that had run for about fourteen years and left the industry facing dues, with interest and penalties, of more than Rs 92,000 crore in the original estimate. Court filings put Vodafone Idea's share at about Rs 28,309 crore at the time, a figure that grew as interest and penalties were added.

The next month, Vodafone Idea reported a net loss of Rs 50,922 crore for the July to September quarter, then the largest quarterly loss in Indian corporate history. In December 2019, Kumar Mangalam Birla warned that the company might have to shut down if there was no relief on statutory dues.

In September 2020, the Supreme Court gave operators ten years to pay. In 2021, the government announced a reform package that included a moratorium on these payments and the option to convert interest into equity. In February 2023, the government converted Rs 16,133 crore of interest dues into shares, becoming Vodafone Idea's largest single shareholder with about a third of the company. In April 2025, it converted Rs 36,950 crore of spectrum dues, taking its stake to 48.99 per cent.

In October 2025, the Supreme Court allowed the government to reconsider Vodafone Idea's AGR dues. On 30 April 2026, the DoT communicated a revised figure: Rs 64,046 crore, as of 31 December 2025, down from the earlier provisional figure of Rs 87,695 crore. Payment begins in FY2031-32 at a minimum of Rs 100 crore a year for four years, with the remainder paid in six equal annual instalments up to FY2040-41.

Put together, this is not a simple tale of a heavy-handed regulator. It is a story of a disputed definition, a court ruling, and then a long series of government interventions that effectively turned the state into a major shareholder of a private telco it did not want to see fail.

## The Numbers Don't Lie: Vodafone Idea's Decline on Paper

The numbers below come from company disclosures and TRAI data, matched as closely as possible by period. A few rules apply. Subscribers reported by TRAI and by the company differ in definition, so the two sets are not mixed. Borrowings that already include deferred spectrum and AGR obligations are not added to those obligations again.

Subscribers first. The merged company began with nearly 408 million customers in August 2018. By June 2020, its base had fallen to about 280 million. In September 2026 it reported 194.4 million subscribers, of whom 131 million used 4G or 5G. The company says subscriber additions turned net positive from February 2026, which matters more than any single number: it suggests the bleeding has slowed, not that the wound has closed.

TRAI's data for August 2026 shows how the market has rearranged itself. Of roughly 1,293 million wireless connections, Jio held about 508 million, Airtel about 492 million, Vodafone Idea about 200 million and BSNL about 94 million. On those counts, Vi's share of the mobile market is about 15 per cent, against about 39 per cent for Jio and 38 per cent for Airtel.

|> "Vi love to see you together." Reliance Jio, on X, welcoming the Vi rebrand in September 2020

Average revenue per user tells a slightly kinder story. Vi's ARPU has risen for many consecutive quarters as the industry raised tariffs in successive rounds, the latest in July 2024. It reached Rs 198 in the September 2026 quarter. Airtel reported Rs 271 and Jio Rs 218.8 for the same quarter, so Vi still earns less per customer than either rival.

Revenue and profit. For FY2024-25, Vi reported revenue of Rs 43,571 crore, EBITDA of Rs 18,127 crore and a net loss of Rs 20,217 crore. For FY2025-26, revenue rose 3 per cent to Rs 44,873 crore, and EBITDA reached Rs 19,003 crore. The company reported a net profit of Rs 34,552 crore for that year, but this came almost entirely from a one-time accounting gain of Rs 55,622 crore after the AGR reassessment. Strip that out and the business was still losing money. In the September 2026 quarter, it reported a net loss of Rs 5,975 crore on revenue of Rs 11,884 crore.

Obligations. At the end of September 2026, Vi reported deferred spectrum dues of Rs 1,31,268 crore, AGR dues on its books of Rs 26,264 crore, bank debt of only Rs 943 crore and a negative net worth of Rs 44,298 crore. Most of what it owes, in other words, is owed to the government.

Investment. Capital expenditure in FY2025-26 was Rs 8,742 crore. It rose sharply to Rs 3,946 crore in the September 2026 quarter alone, and the company says 5G is now live in around 400 cities and towns across its 17 priority circles, with 4G reaching nearly 88 per cent of the population.

What each metric means is worth one line. Subscribers show reach. ARPU shows pricing power. EBITDA shows whether the core business earns cash before financing. Net loss shows whether it covers all its costs. Capex shows whether it is building for tomorrow. Vi is improving on ARPU, EBITDA and capex, still weak on profit, and still far behind on reach.

## The Final Verdict: When Brand Equity Meets Business Reality

Hutch built a distinctive identity and a memorable promise of network reliability, carried by a pug and a pink logo.

Vodafone inherited that equity, added global scale and a red identity, and gave the brand a new creative vocabulary in the ZooZoos, while keeping the pug as its soul.

![The global red, on a shop door. The name India knew for thirteen years. (Photo: Vodafone signage)](images/vodafone-sign.jpg)

Idea contributed customers, spectrum, a footprint beyond the metros and a separate brand legacy that made its own name a compliment.

Jio changed the economics of the market, raised consumer expectations of what a data connection should cost and accelerated a consolidation that was coming anyway.

The internal vulnerabilities mattered as much as the external shock: a heavy balance sheet, limited investment capacity during the most expensive years, and the slow, unglamorous labour of integration. The AGR ruling then arrived on top of all of it.

In September 2020, the merged company unified its brands as Vi, read as "we", with the line "Together for Tomorrow".

॥ "Together for Tomorrow." Two loved brands, retired into one shorter name.

|> "It's time for a fresh start." Ravinder Takkar, MD and CEO, Vodafone Idea, at the Vi launch, September 2020

Vi still operates. It serves nearly two hundred million people, its losses are narrowing before one-time items, its ARPU is rising and its investment is picking up. Fresh capital, the reduced AGR burden and continued government backing could change its trajectory. Its decline was not inevitable, and its future is not written.

## Closing Footnote: The Pug Was Never the Problem

The pug did its job. So did the ZooZoos, and so did Sirji. Each made an invisible service feel human, memorable and worth choosing.

But an advertisement cannot buy spectrum. It cannot raise a tower, lay fibre or pay a licence fee. Consumer affection, brand equity, customer experience and commercial viability are four different things, and a brand team that confuses them will one day be surprised.

![Together we can: the parent group's global line. In India, the bill for "together" came in rupees. (Advertisement: Vodafone Group)](images/vodafone-together-we-can.jpg)

So here is a Monday Morning Audit for any brand manager, borrowed from a pug.

A/. What are we promising? Write it in one sentence, the way Hutch did.

B/. What do customers remember? Not what we said, but what they still repeat years later.

C/. Does the experience deliver? A promise of following everywhere means nothing in a dead zone.

D/. Can the business sustainably fund the promise? If not, the most beautiful advertisement in the country is writing a cheque the balance sheet cannot cash.

![Two ZooZoos, a bouquet and a cardboard Eiffel Tower. Romance is easy; the network is the hard part. (Advertisement: Vodafone)](images/zoozoo-proposal.png)

The boy still crosses the bridge in our memories, and the pug still follows. It was a lovely promise. It just turned out that keeping it cost a great deal more than a dog food bill.
